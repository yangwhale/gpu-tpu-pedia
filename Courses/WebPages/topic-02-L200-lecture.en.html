This lecture adds a third: bandwidth. And as we'll see, the one that decides whether an op is fast is often neither of the first two.

“The third is bandwidth” is the seam to the last lecture and the lead-in to Section 1. Say it and move on. Section 1 derives the exchange rate itself. ⭐ Scroll on to the second yellow box. Again, don't click.

⭐ Read it and stop. Don't explain why. Point at the last line of the answer area and read it. The numbers are out; the “why” is an IOU. ⛔ Classic mistake: explaining it on the spot. Scroll one screen to the Section 0 figure. It has just two boxes. Nothing else to find. Make those two boxes clear, then move on.

Here's the first figure. It's the lecture's only premise: these two boxes.

On the left, the GPU. It started as a graphics processor. From day one it had to serve tons of small, unrelated jobs. It doesn't know the shapes, the access patterns, or the control flow. So most of its die goes to handling any case: the cache hierarchy, the warp schedulers, the huge thread counts.

On the right, the TPU. It started as an ASIC built for matrix multiply, serving only neural networks from day one. Shapes are regular, access is predictable, control flow is static. So it needs none of that “handle any case” circuitry.

⛔ Easiest slip (I've made it myself): don't say “all the saved area went into compute.”
The savings became neither compute nor on-chip storage. Section 3b rules out both with two tables: the GPU has 16% more total MACs and 73% more on-chip SRAM.

So what did it save? Complexity itself. No cache, no scheduler, layout fixed at compile time. The same compute in four big blocks, not 592 small ones. That's 148 times fewer sets of control circuitry.

⚠️ Someone will likely ask “So is it more power-efficient?” There's a solid official answer. Give it with its caveats.

Yes, you can answer. Two things suffice. First, a quote from the first TPU paper. It lists everything left out: no caches, no branch prediction, no out-of-order execution, no multithreading, no context switching. The next sentence: “Minimalism is a virtue of domain-specific processors.”

⭐ Second, a number, which beats any adjective: “On that die, control logic takes just 2%.”
Data buffers 37%, compute 30%, I/O 10%. Control: two percent. The paper adds that this part is much larger on CPUs and GPUs. ⛔ Then brake. All three caveats, every time:
One: the paper compares the 2015 TPU v1 with the K80. Back then the savings did become compute: a small die with 25× the MACs. For v7 vs. B200, Section 3b counts the GPU at 16% more. Same mechanism; ten years later, the opposite outcome.
Two: Ironwood's official 2× perf per watt is vs. the previous TPU, not GPUs.
Three: yield and reliability. No source, so don't say it. The official word is “deterministic execution”: tail latency, not reliability. Don't mix them. One beat. Next two lines: the takeaway.

One doesn't know what you'll run, so it hedges at every step. The other knows from day one, so it hedges nothing.

No more scrolling. Stay on the two boxes.

So what is this lecture about? The two sides differ everywhere. But every difference follows from a single decision.

Which decision? Moving a block of data from HBM onto the chip: who schedules it. On the GPU, a cache guesses for you at runtime. The TPU has no cache; it's fixed at compile time.

Shapes padded to 8 by 128, how big a bite one instruction takes, who writes the kernels, even what wiring a whole machine costs: it all grows from that one decision.

Slow down. This is the lecture's claim; every later section is evidence for it. Rush it and it sounds like a slogan.

One more thing, and I mean it: this lecture doesn't rank the chips. Both origins were the right call at the time, and each still carries its cost. You won't hear “which is better” today. Only “who decides this, on this side.”

That's the foundation. Straight into Section 1. It's one division, so it's quick. After that, Section 2 starts taking the hardware apart.

Likely questions

“Only one cause”: isn't that oversimplifying?

There are a dozen-odd symptoms. “One” means one cause. Caches, shape alignment, who writes kernels, interconnect wiring: all differ, but they're not a dozen parallel facts. They're a dozen consequences of one decision.

At the opening it's a claim, not a proven conclusion. Say so: “You don't have to believe it yet. Sections 3 and 5 each test it.” That convinces more than digging in, and gives them a reason to keep listening.

Does the TPU really have no cache at all? Then what is VMEM?

Precisely: the TPU has no hardware-managed cache tier. It has on-chip storage, plenty of it, but as a software-managed scratchpad: when data arrives, where it goes and when it leaves are all fixed by the compiler. Nothing decides anything at runtime.

That distinction is the main thread, so the question is a gift: “Right. What's missing isn't capacity, it's who decides.”
⚠️ Don't go into VMEM size or partitioning at the opening. Section 3 has a figure for it.

Why make FlashAttention the star? Why not a GEMM, or a whole model?

It meets two conditions that rarely coincide.

First, it runs on one chip. No inter-chip traffic, so the story stays on one line (hence Section 4, on inter-chip links, never mentions it).

Second, it hits four things at once: memory access, shapes, on-chip capacity, kernel authorship. A pure GEMM is too clean to touch the last three. A whole model is too messy: the subject changes every step; nothing lands.

Section 4 has nothing to do with FlashAttention. Why is it in the core track?

It really is off the FlashAttention thread. The test is hard: FlashAttention appears 0 times in Section 4's main text (grepped). It finishes on one chip and never touches inter-chip links.

But it underpins Section 6's measurements: those numbers come from 64 vs. 64 chips, wired in structurally different ways. Without it, Section 6's ratio can't be read. So it stays in the core track; don't skip it whole. Short on time? Two sentences (emergency list, top).

Now we can name the real protagonist of this lecture.

Both calculations said one thing: whether an op is fast depends on operations per byte moved. So there's one way to make a slow op fast: shrink the denominator. Move less.

And the best way to move less is to merge adjacent ops into one kernel, so intermediates never touch HBM. That's fusion.

Let it land: it doesn't save a single FLOP. Only the denominator changes. Many think fusion means computing less. It doesn't.

1.2 Square is a special case: the smallest dimension sets intensity (Figure 1-8)

The matmuls in those examples were square: all sides N, giving N/3. But a real matmul is M×K · K×N. So which dimension sets the intensity?

Same division. Operations: 2 M N K. Bytes moved: 2 times MK plus KN plus MN. It simplifies cleanly: one over intensity equals one over M, plus one over N, plus one over K.

Say “reciprocals add” on its own, once. It's the key: reciprocals add, so the smallest one dominates.

So “longer K, higher intensity” is right, and monotonic. But there's a ceiling. Column ②'s five rows show it: M and N fixed at 512, K from 64 up to a million, and intensity climbs from 51 to 256, then stops. The cap is MN over M plus N: exactly 256.

K can't rescue a small M or N. The harshest case: decode at batch size 1. M is 1, so intensity caps at 1, however big K and N get. That one line is why LLM decode is always bandwidth-bound.

Now attention. In Q times K transpose, both outer dimensions are the sequence length S, and the contraction dimension is head_dim. Most models use 128, so intensity is about 128. Below v7's 312 line, and v6e's 560.

⭐ Say it aloud; it overturns a common assumption: “Attention is a matmul, so of course it's compute-bound” is a myth. Unfused, it sits on the bandwidth side, pinned by head_dim.

So what does Flash change? Direction now; Section 2's first figure does the math: it saves no FLOPs. It changes the denominator. The S×S matrix never touches HBM. Only Q, K, V and O cross it, so intensity becomes S over 2. With Wan's S of 75,600, that's 37,800.

⭐ With 148 SMs, is shared memory's line of 64 an approximation summing all SMs?

Script · 5.1 Who owns latency

The GPU's answer: one warp stalls, run the next. In this Gantt chart, dashed boxes are “waiting for data,” while other warps keep issuing.

“This only hides the wait if enough warps are resident. That's all ‘occupancy’ means.”

The TPU differs: the schedule is fixed at compile time, unchangeable at runtime.

How can it be that precise? Latency is a constant. No cache, so latency is constant. Constant, so every cycle can be filled at compile time.

Script · 5.4 Catching the objection

Scroll to Figure P-33. Two minutes. Defend only.

Right about here, the strongest objection always comes up, and half of it is right: “Doesn't CUDA Graph make the GPU compile-time too?”

⭐ First, grant the right half in full. All the persuasion is here: “You record the whole step's kernel graph up front and run it straight through. Real gains: on many workloads, on vs. off is an order of magnitude.”

To judge what it overturns, be exact about why it's fast. The usual story is wrong.

It doesn't save “branches.” It saves the CPU launching kernels one by one. One launch is about 5–10 microseconds on the host; an MoE step has tens of thousands of kernels. Launching alone takes tens of milliseconds. A graph makes it all one replay.

Point at the figure's middle column. That's the answer.

It comes down to which layer. Split it in three: sequencing (which kernel next), placement (which SM gets a block), issue (which instruction next cycle).

CUDA Graph freezes only the first layer. The other two are untouched. Each graph node is still a normal kernel launch. Schedulers, scoreboards, warp slots, resident contexts: all still working.
The TPU freezes all three, the third down to the exact cycle and issue slot.

Three reasons. Stress the third: “Hardware cost is set by the worst case it must support, not the one you run. Even if 100% of workloads used graphs, none of §3.2's five components could go. At tape-out, no chip knows if you'll use graphs.”

So the real point runs the other way: for this performance, the GPU has to become more like a TPU. And it can only move the top layer earlier. The two below can't move. If they could, those transistors would be long gone.

Yes, but be clear on its nature: the cost moved; it wasn't added.

On the GPU, the scheduler does it live every run, spreading the cost over execution; the TPU pays once, at compile time.

⭐ Mention what it buys: very stable runs. The same GPU code can vary a lot run to run; TPU runs barely do.

⚠️ Any shape change means a recompile. A real pain point. Full porting bill: L300 §5.5. Don't open it here.

If I turn CUDA Graph on everywhere, are the GPU's five components just dead weight?

Good question; the answer is this section's hardest point: dead weight you still carry.

Hardware cost is set by the worst case it must support, not the case you run. At tape-out, the chip doesn't know whether you'll use graphs.

Even with graphs, only the top layer freezes. Which SM gets a block, which instruction issues next: still decided at runtime. Schedulers and warp slots keep working.

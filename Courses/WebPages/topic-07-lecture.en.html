Section 0 · One step got 50% faster. Do you believe it? (4′)Planned 4′ · with interaction 3.9′

🖥 Main screen for this segment: course page Section 0 · One step got 50% faster. Do you believe it?

🎯 What this segment must leave behind: good news? Don't celebrate it or doubt it yet. Spend a minute estimating: is this number possible?

🧭 Get everyone there first: “We're in Section 0 of the course page, ‘One step got 50% faster. Do you believe it?’” Switch there, let everyone find it, then begin.

🖥 Screen: switch to 0.1 Two step times. Point at the two step times, then the 1.50 line.

A story from our own work. We were training Tencent Hunyuan3, Hy3 for short. One day we changed a set of parameters and reran. A step dropped from 11.76 seconds to 7.84. We'll call the old config the original recipe. 11.76 divided by 7.84 is 1.5. Same time, half again as many tokens.

Just a lucky run? We ran the same config twice more: 7.846 and 7.840. Under a tenth of a percent apart: not noise.

💬 Interaction: ask the audience: do you believe this number? Hands up three times: believe, don't, not sure; then one reason per group. Once you hear “new parameters, of course it's faster” and “half again at once is too much,” move on. Don't judge yet.

🖥 Screen: still on 0.1. Point at the “Setup” table below the step times, rows top to bottom.

First, what this ran on. Hy3 is an MoE model with 295 billion parameters and 80 layers. Every layer except the first has 192 experts. An expert is a feed-forward network; each token picks only 8.

The machine is 64 TPU v7 chips. Each chip has two TensorCores, which the profile counts as two devices, so 128 in all. The weights don't fit on one device. So they're split into 64 shards, one per device, and gathered back from the others when needed. That's FSDP; it comes up throughout.

We train in FP8, one byte per number, on open-source MaxText.

🖥 Screen: switch to 0.2 What changed; point at its three rows, top to bottom.

So what changed? We diffed both configs line by line: only 13 lines differ. Twelve are expert-matmul tile sizes. A kernel is the code the chip actually runs. A tile is how big a block it loads into on-chip memory to compute at once. Going by the config, they went from 1024 to 2048 and 1536.

The last line, use_tokamax_gmm, goes from True to False: expert matmuls drop the tokamax library's kernel for MaxText's own.

A new implementation, bigger tiles, faster matmuls. Completely plausible, and at the time we saw nothing wrong either. But we weren't sure. Really faster, or had something gone wrong? I couldn't tell. When you're not sure, don't rush to open the profile, the run's record of every op's start and end. First, spend a minute estimating.

🖥 Screen: stop on the ⭐ line in 0.3 When in doubt, estimate first.

Estimate what? Just one question: for this machine and model type, is a 50% faster FP8 training step normal? With that range, you know what to look for in the profile.

⚠️ Only if asked (not part of the talk)
  • Asked “Is the 50% about throughput or step time?”: throughput. 11,758.2 ÷ 7,842.1 = 1.499; the step is one third shorter (0.667). 0.1 collapsible block.
  • Asked “Do profile step times match the logs?”: profile median steps, 11,758.2 / 7,842.1 ms; MaxText's host timer: 11.765 / 7.853 s, including dispatch, so about 0.1% longer. Reruns: 7,846.1 / 7,840.1 ms. All in the 0.1 collapsible block.
  • Asked “Is tile 1024 what the original recipe really used?”: no. On tokamax the launch script injects the original's tile separately; the compiled program says (512, 2048, 1536). So “1024 → 2048” doesn't mean the kernel's real tiling changed (0.2 collapsible block).
  • Asked “Why 64 shards but 128 devices?”: besides 64-way FSDP there's a data axis of 2; 64 × 2 = 128, so each shard sits on two devices (Section 3, memory).
  • Asked “How was 295B checked?”: the tencent/Hy3 model card on Hugging Face lists Total 295B and the MTP layer's 3.8B on two separate rows (its layer row says 80 layers, excluding the MTP layer); Section 3 back-calculates 298.85B from memory, exactly the two added together.
  • Terms like gmm, tile, FSDP, all-gather: the collapsible block “Unfamiliar term?” ending 0.3 has ten. Explain each as it comes up; don't read the list.
  • ⛔ In this section, don't judge or hint at the conclusion. “Believe it or not” only gets the audience to place a bet and give a reason; Section 1's rulers decide.

Section 1 · Estimate first: is this number even possible? (10′)Planned 10′ · with interaction 8.8′

🖥 Main screen for this segment: course page Section 1 · Estimate first: is this number even possible? · main figure Same switch to FP8…

🎯 What this segment must leave behind: DeepSeek-V3 (MoE) gains only about 20% from FP8; the modified run got a dense model's gain. Suspicious: find the 3.9 s in the profile.

🧭 Get everyone there first: “We're in Section 1 of the course page, ‘Estimate first: is this number even possible?’” Switch there, let everyone find it, then begin.

Estimates take a ruler. Ruler one is MFU: a chip's real floating-point operations per second, divided by its peak. TFLOP/s is trillions per second. A v7 chip's BF16 peak is 2,307 TFLOP/s. BF16 is two bytes a number, the most common in training.

For FP8 we divide by the same number. FLOPs come from the model's shapes: one multiply-add is two operations, however many bytes a number takes. The denominator doesn't change either. So both precisions sit on one ruler.

🖥 Screen: switch to the table in 1.1 Ruler one: MFU. Point at the two public MoE rows, Hy3 BF16 and the original, the red row, then the 405B at the bottom.

Public MoEs Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V3 both sit near 27%. Hy3 BF16 is 28.7, the FP8 original 29.3: same cluster. The modified row leaps to 44, just shy of dense Llama 405B.

Where does it come from? MaxText logs each step's FLOPs: 3,981.54 TFLOP per device. Two devices per chip: times 2, then divide by step time. Both FP8 runs log identical FLOPs, so the whole MFU gain is the shorter step. MFU alone says the number jumped, not whether it should have.

🖥 Screen: open the collapsible block “MFU denominators” under 1.1; point at 4,614.

A common trap here: FP8 peak is twice BF16's, 4,614 per chip. Use that as the denominator, and the modified run's MFU drops to 22%, artificially halved. Same run, new denominator, new MFU. So for FP8 MFU, always name the peak you divided by.

🖥 Screen: open the collapsible block “Compute it yourself” under 1.1. Of its eight code lines, point at the step-time and FLOPs ones, then 676.2 and 1,013.7.

Don't take our word for this ruler; measure it yourself. MaxText logs step times and FLOPs as TensorBoard events. Eight lines of Python read them, take the median step, double and divide. Our two runs: 676.2 and 1,013.7, 0.1% off the table, only from picking different steps.

🖥 Screen: switch to the “Five conventions” table at the end of 1.1. Skim only the “Pitfall” column of rows 1–4; row 5, HBM, isn't used until Section 3: skip it.

We use these conventions throughout; we hit or nearly hit each pitfall. Step time is a median, to dodge the step slowed by writing the profile. FLOPs come from the shapes, FP8 included. The denominator: always 2,307. One-line sanity check: MFU above 100% means a convention is wrong.

💬 Interaction: ask the audience: FP8 makes a number one byte, not two. At most how much faster is one matmul? A whole step? Accept “2×” or “the step too”; don't correct. Move on at the first answer; the roofline settles it.

🖥 Screen: switch to the 1.2 figure “FP8: at most 2×, and only on matmuls”. Left only: slope, flat roof, ridge point; then the example point, blue roof to orange.

Ruler two, the roofline, gives an op's top speed. The x-axis is arithmetic intensity, operations per byte moved from HBM. On the left slope, HBM bandwidth sets the speed; on the flat roof to the right, peak compute does. They meet at the ridge point.

Switch to FP8, and both ends double. A matmul on the flat roof gets a roof twice as high. A matmul on the slope moves half the bytes, so its point shifts 2 times right. Up the slope, that's exactly 2 times faster too. So one matmul gets at most 2 times faster.

But a step isn't just matmuls. Routing, permuting tokens by expert, elementwise ops, communication: none are on this chart, and FP8 doesn't help them. So, that question: one matmul tops out at 2 times, so a whole step can't reach 2 times.

🖥 Screen: same figure. Point at the right-hand curve and the red dot above it. Skip the takeaway band below for now: it answers a later interaction.

The right curve is our own derivation. Say matmuls are a share p of the step. At best, FP8 halves that share. So a step is at most 1 divided by (1 minus p over 2) times faster: 2 times only at p equals 100%.

Note the baseline: Hy3's own BF16, not Section 0's original, since others all report BF16-to-FP8 gains. The modified run is 1.53 times Hy3 BF16. Working back, at least 70% of the step must be matmuls.

The same formula on others gives a reference. DeepSeek-V3 with FP8 is 1.214 times its BF16, so matmuls are at least 35% of its step. The dense 405B is about 1.53 times: 70% again. A dense layer is just a few big matmuls, so 70% is believable.

💬 Interaction: ask the audience: every layer of an MoE also routes, permutes tokens and gathers expert weights from other devices. Can matmuls be 70% of its step? On “no” or “not sure,” switch.

🖥 Screen: switch to the 1.3 figure “Same switch to FP8…”. Point at dense +52.8% and DeepSeek-V3 +21.4% up top; Hy3 Original +2.2%; the red row, +53.2%.

Now peers: same chip, same FP8 switch. The dense 405B gains 52.8%. DeepSeek-V3, an MoE, only 21.4%. Hy3's own original: only 2.2% over its BF16. The modified run: 53.2%, like the dense model.

The three Hy3 points are our own measurements on 64 chips; the other rows cite published ones. The ruler isn't precise, but it measures one thing: what a change like this usually gains on similar models.

💬 Interaction: ask the audience: recall Section 0's show of hands? Bet again: an MoE got a dense model's gain. Is the change that good, or was something missed? Mind changed? Hands up. No reveal; carry the bets on.

Either this change cured all of MoE's old problems in one go, or something's wrong. The estimate can't say which. But now we know what to look for in the profile.

🖥 Screen: switch to the two-column table in 1.4 Open the profile with questions in hand.

Back to Section 0's step times: the missing 3.9 seconds must come off some ops. If it's real, the same ops are all there, just shorter, mostly expert matmuls. Communication is unchanged. If not, a whole class of ops is gone or absurdly short, and communication shrinks too.

This table isn't a conclusion; it's a checklist. Left column matches, we celebrate. Right column matches, we dig. Take it with you, and open the profile.

⚠️ Only if asked (not part of the talk)
  • Asked “Which MFU denominator is right?”: 2,307 is the per-chip BF16 peak (MaxText and this course use it). XProf's Roofline Model page gives 1,028.75 per TensorCore; × 2 = 2,057.5, 10.8% smaller. Dividing by the FP8 peak, 4,614, artificially halves MFU (1,014.8 ÷ 4,614 = 22.0%). Always state FP8 MFU's denominator (1.1 collapsible block).
  • Asked “How do you get p ≥ 70%?”: new step time = p/2 + (1 − p); a measured speedup of s gives p ≥ 2 × (1 − 1/s). DeepSeek-V3: s = 1.214 → 35%; 405B and Hy3 modified: s ≈ 1.53 → 69%. It's our derivation's upper bound, not a measurement (1.2 collapsible block).
  • Asked “The original gathers expert weights in FP8, halving those bytes: FP8 touches more than matmuls?”: yes, but the original's exposed communication is only about 0.2 s a step (195.9 ms; whole-step accounting, 2.5 collapsible block “Reading start/done…”). Counting it leaves p's lower bound unchanged (1.2 collapsible block).
  • Asked “Why is it 1.53× here and 1.50× in Section 0?”: different baselines. 1.53 = modified 1,014.8 ÷ Hy3 BF16 662.3 (“BF16 to FP8”, like the public table); 1.50 = original ÷ modified step time. BF16 and FP8 batches differ (12 vs 7), so compare TFLOP/s per chip, not step times.
  • The public measurements (Qwen3, DeepSeek-V3, 405B) are cited from the Hy3 tuning log, not measured by this course; if pressed on the source, say so.
  • ⛔ No “it's fake” verdict here; say only “suspicious; we know what to look for.”

00

Fix the ruler: how this course does the math

⏱ 3 min
What this segment must leave behind Fix the ruler first, then measure, and never swap it midway. Students must accept: no conclusions here, only math, with the assumptions nailed down first.

Script

Stay on the first screen. Don't scroll yet.

First, the method, because it's not how most material on large models works.

Almost everything out there hands you conclusions. MoE saves compute. MLA saves memory. FlashAttention is faster. All true. But you heard them. And the trouble with things you heard: change the setting, and you can't tell if they still hold.

This lecture does one thing: the math.

How? We take a real model, DeepSeek V3, and follow one token all the way from input to output. At every component, we ask the same four questions on the spot. How big are the weights? How big are the activations? How many multiplications? And the fourth, the one that matters most: does anything blow up here?

You'll find that last question is usually the point.

One beat. The real content of this segment starts here.

But to measure, you need a ruler first. The usual mistake in this kind of math isn't an arithmetic error. It's switching rulers halfway. So we nail the assumptions down now, and we don't touch them again.

Scroll to the four “Assumptions” cards.

Five of them. One: sequence length is 128K. That's 131,072 tokens, roughly a short book. Two: batch is one. Get a single sequence right first; multiply later. Three: precision is bf16, two bytes per number. Four: forward only, no backward. Five: the yardstick is one TPU v7 device, 94.74 GiB. Every number we compute gets divided by it. Otherwise you have no feel for it.

Number four is the one that needs explaining. Why forward only?

Optionally ask “Anyone want to guess why?”, then answer yourself.

Because the forward pass is fact, and the backward pass is mixed with decisions.

Forward is matmul after matmul. The size of each step is fixed. There's no choice involved. Backward is different. First you decide: do you keep the intermediate results, or recompute them when needed? Which device holds the optimizer state? Do you accumulate gradients over a few steps before each update? Those are strategies. People choose differently, and the numbers come out completely different.

Mix decisions into facts and the math gets muddy. So this lecture only counts the part everyone has to pay. Backward gets Topic 4. By then we'll know how to do the math, and strategy will be worth discussing.

If the room looks impatient, jump to the “First, guess a number” quiz, then come back for the assumptions.

OK, the ruler's set. Now, Step 1: how a word turns into a row of numbers.

Likely questions

Why 128K? Why not 4K or 1M?

Because 128K is today's norm, and it sits right at the tipping point. At 4K many problems stay hidden: attention takes only about 10% of the compute (computed later). At 128K it takes about 80%, and the bottleneck has moved entirely.

At the key moment, drag the slider at the top in front of them.

Batch of 1? Isn't that unrealistic?

On purpose. Batch is a multiplier: it scales every term equally and only hides the proportions.

Batch=1 shows the structure. For realism, move the slider at the top to 8.

What's bf16? Why not fp32 or fp8?

bf16 is today's default precision for training and inference: two bytes per number. fp32 is four bytes, more precise but twice the cost. fp8 is one byte, cheaper but numerically risky.

Stop there. Precision is Topic 8; “two bytes per number” is all the math needs.

Where does 94.74 come from?

It's the usable HBM of one TPU v7 device: a hardware constant, not something we measured.

If pressed on what's inside the device: that's Topic 2. Note it and move on, or it eats your time.

Where does 576 come from? Why not 512?

512 is the compressed KV, 64 the RoPE slice. Together they're what one token stores per layer. In the config: kv_lora_rank = 512 and qk_rope_head_dim = 64.

Why can't the 64 RoPE dimensions be compressed too? Answer in one line, “they carry position; compress them and they're useless,” and stop. Step 2 covers this asymmetry; expanding now blows the time.

MQA needs only 3.81 GiB, even less than MLA. Why not just use MQA?

The best question you'll get: it's the crux of Step 2. Don't answer it fully yet.

Say only this: MQA saves by making every head share one copy of K/V, and the expressiveness it loses is gone for good. MLA saves almost as much without making any head pay. Step 2 is the how.

Then park it on the board; point back to it at MLA. An open hook in the opening is worth more than an answered question.

So how much does backward cost? Can you give a rough number now?

Give a directional answer to settle the room: compute roughly triples, and the biggest chunk of memory is actually optimizer state, larger than the model itself.

Then stop: the line signals “more math is coming,” not Topic 4.

What not to say

The three easiest ways to drift here ① Don't start explaining what a Transformer is. Newcomers pick it up along the “follow the token” thread; a crash course takes ten minutes and wrecks the pace.
② Don't explain bf16's bit layout. Say “two bytes” and move on.
③ Don't linger on “why 94.74.” That's Topic 2.
Pacing This segment must be done in three minutes. It's the foundation, not the house. What grabs them is the Step 1 moment: “we haven't entered layer 1, and a chunk of HBM is already gone.” Get there early.

The model card: how to read the table

⏱ 2 min
What this segment must leave behind Every number in this table comes from the officially published config file. None is estimated, and none is something we measured. It decides whether the room believes your later math. Say it out loud.

Script

Scroll to the three “The model card” tables.

The table has over twenty rows. Don't read them one by one. People tune out, and won't remember them on first hearing anyway.

Pull out just four numbers now. Point back to the rest when they come up.

Click each of the next four on the table as you say it.

First: hidden_size is 7168. That's the width of the residual stream, the main road running through the whole model. Every activation we compute later is the token count times this number. If the room can remember only one number, make it this one.

Second: 61 layers. Nothing unusual there. What's unusual is the next row: first_k_dense_replace is 3. That means the first 3 layers are ordinary MLPs, and MoE starts at layer 4. So, precisely, it's “3 dense layers plus 58 MoE layers.” We'll use that 3 and 58 again and again when counting parameters.

The third number is actually a false. tie_word_embeddings = false. It means the big lookup matrix at the entrance and the big matrix at the exit that turns vectors back into words don't share weights. They're two separate copies. Many models share them to save a copy. V3 doesn't, and pays over 900 million extra parameters.

Plant a hook here. Don't answer it.

You can ask in passing: “Why do some models share, and some don't?” Then say, “We'll get to that at the exit, in the last step.” Not now.

The fourth isn't a number. It's a sentence, the most important one here:

Every number in this table comes from DeepSeek's officially published config file. None is estimated, and none is something we measured ourselves. Every derivation that follows starts from this table.

Say it: at the final tally, we'll get 671 billion total parameters and 36.6 billion active, matching the officially published figures. That's when this sentence pays off.

Likely questions

How was the width 7168 chosen?

Two hard factors. It must align with the hardware's matrix units (7168 is a multiple of 128), and balance against the layer count: same parameter budget, wider or deeper? Every model redoes it; there's no standard answer.

⚠️ Don't say “7168 has to be divisible by the head count.” That doesn't hold for V3. 128 heads at 128 dims each make attention 16,384 dims wide inside, 2.29× of 7168, and o_proj folds it back down. In standard MHA the two numbers must be equal; MLA removes that constraint.

That's also why o_proj is the largest weight in an MLA layer (117.44M, 63% of the layer). It spans the 16384 → 7168 gap. Point back to this at the five-row table in Step 2.

Why is the vocabulary such an odd number, 129,280?

What's certain: vocabulary size is set when the tokenizer is trained, and it's a trade-off. Bigger: one word is one token, so sequences are shorter and faster, but both big matrices at the entrance and exit grow. Smaller: the reverse. 129,280 sits somewhere in between.

What you can check: 129,280 ÷ 128 = 1,010, exactly, a friendly shape for the matrix units.

Be honest about the unknown: DeepSeek has never publicly explained how this number was chosen. If asked, say “They haven't said; I'm not sure.” Don't improvise a plausible reason.

What's the MTP row?

num_nextn_predict_layers = 1: besides predicting the next token, the model also predicts the one after that. A mention is enough; Step 6 goes into it. Its uses in training and inference differ completely; that's the interesting part.

Why aren't the first 3 layers MoE?

Good question, but hold it. Never answer offhand “because the early layers are more general.” That explanation is everywhere, but it isn't the paper's reason.

For now, one line: the earliest public reason was training stability (DeepSeekMoE observed that load balancing in the first layer converges especially slowly), and the paper doesn't explain why V3 went from 1 layer to 3.

Step 3 takes it apart, with its own figure.

What not to say

The two easiest ways to drift here ① Don't explain the fields one by one, especially MLA's six (q_lora_rank, kv_lora_rank and the rest). They only make sense in Step 2. Explain them now and you explain twice; the first time won't land.
② Don't explain YaRN. If someone asks about the max_position_embeddings row, say “an extrapolation method stretches it from 4K to about 160K; that's Topic 3” and move on.
Pacing Two minutes. Hand out the model card as a reference for later; don't walk through the model. Past three minutes, you've drifted.
02

How a word becomes a row of numbers

⏱ 8 min
What this segment must leave behind Before the first layer, a chunk of HBM is already gone. Nothing has been “computed” yet, just one table lookup, but 3.48 GiB is already spent. It's the lecture's first “so that's how the bill builds up” moment.

Script

Scroll to “Step 1 · From text to vectors”: vocabulary matrix on the left, residual-stream tensor on the right, a “lookup” arrow between.

First, what actually happens at the input. A sentence comes in and gets cut into tokens.

A token isn't a character, and it isn't a word. Common Chinese characters are mostly one token each; a long English word may be split into two or three pieces. Once it's cut, every token is just an integer ID. On V3 that ID runs from 0 to 129,279, because the vocabulary is 129,280.

Scroll to the axis figure under “Tokenization: why 129,280”: green band on top, red below, axis between: the tug-of-war over vocabulary size. Talk to the figure. Don't read the text.

The room usually asks first: “where does 129,280 come from?” The answer first: it's not special at all. Look at this axis. Llama 2 is 32K. Llama 3 is 128K, V3 is 129K, Qwen3 is 152K, gpt-oss about 200K, and Gemma 2 is 256K. V3 and Llama 3 are practically the same point.

Point at the green bandA bigger vocabulary buys you this: the same sentence cuts into fewer tokens. Shorter sequences, and the square term in attention drops with them. That pays off in Step 2.

Point at the red bandIt costs you three things: both tables get bigger, rare words get too few samples to learn well, and the exit layer computes softmax over the whole vocabulary.

Get this one exactly right. The “both tables get bigger” cost barely applies to V3. Table size depends only on vocabulary × width. It has nothing to do with the 61 layers. So the bigger the model, the smaller its share: both tables together are only 0.28% of 671B. What really caps vocabulary size is the other two costs.

Pause. Next: this segment's first counterintuitive point.

By default, going from ID to vector isn't a multiplication. It's a lookup.

The model stores a big 129,280 × 7,168 table. Whatever your token's ID is, you just pull out that row. No matmul, no additions. It's fetching one row by index.

It's the plainest step in the whole model, the only one with almost no compute. But not cheap, because of the table itself: 129,280 times 7,168 is 926.68 million parameters, which is 1.73 GiB in bf16.

Point at the “Residual stream” tensor.

Now the second cost. After the lookup, each of the 128K tokens is a 7,168-dimensional vector. Stack them and you get a 131,072 × 7,168 tensor. Two bytes each in bf16: 1.75 GiB.

Add the two together here. Slow down.

A table, 1.73. A tensor, 1.75. Together, 3.48 GiB. And we haven't entered the first layer yet.

This segment lands here. Stop two seconds; let it sink in.

Point at the main road running through the page (the residual stream figure).

One last thing. It shapes how you see the next 61 layers.

That 1.75 GiB tensor isn't a scratch result you throw away. It is the residual stream: one main road running from layer 1 all the way to layer 61. Every layer reads from it once, computes, and writes back to it once.

So 7,168 isn't the internal size of some layer. It's the width of the main road, end to end. That's why the last segment said “if you remember one number, make it this one.” For every later step, the activation memory formula is tokens × 7,168 × bytes per number.

If someone asks how 7,168 was chosen, open the “which half is a hard constraint” block and talk from it. Otherwise leave it shut.

The block in one sentence: 7,168 divides by 128 and by 256. TPU matrix units are 128 or 256 on a side. On NVIDIA, FP16 wants dimensions aligned to 8, and to 64 on A100. Neither side needs padding.

Then add right away: that only explains why it isn't 7,000 or 7,200. It doesn't explain why it isn't 6,144 or 8,192, which divide just as evenly. Wide versus deep: DeepSeek has never publicly said how they decided.

Likely questions

Isn't a lookup the same as multiplying a one-hot vector by a matrix?

Same math; in engineering, worlds apart. A one-hot vector is 129,280 long. Stack 128K tokens and you get a 131,072 × 129,280 matrix. That intermediate alone is 31.56 GiB, and 99.999% of it is zeros.

So the default fetches rows by index: same math, different algorithm, costs orders of magnitude apart. FlashAttention is the same idea.

(Don't say “always”: the page's Expand block notes 34 MaxText production configs turn on use_iota_embed, which really compiles to a one-hot matmul. The denominator is so large that it's only 0.46% of total compute.)

What about position? Is “which token is this” encoded here?

No. On V3 this step carries content only, no position. RoPE injects position on the fly in Step 2's attention: the qk_rope_head_dim = 64 row in the config.

Early Transformers did add a positional encoding to the embedding; mainstream models have mostly moved it into attention. Why is Topic 3. Just mention it.

Does that 1.73 GiB table stay in HBM the whole time?

Yes. It's a weight, resident from load to unload.

The exit table is a separate copy, not the same memory. That's what tie_word_embeddings = false means. Two 926.68M tables, entrance and exit, 3.45 GiB together. This number comes back in Step 6.

If a bigger vocabulary has so many benefits, why not go to a million?

Start with what holds up. A vocabulary scaling-law paper (Scaling Laws with Vocabulary, NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2407.13623) concludes that “most LLMs' vocabularies are too small.” By its fitted formula, Llama2-70B's optimal vocabulary should be 216K or more, against an actual 32K. At the same compute budget, raising the vocabulary from 32K to 43K lifts ARC-Challenge from 29.1 to 32.0.

⚠️ But state the limits: that paper fits dense models from 33M to 3B. There's no basis for extrapolating it to a 671B MoE. It shows the direction. It can't prove 129,280 is optimal for V3.

As for “why not a million”: rare words end up as dead words. It has a name, under-trained / glitch tokens (arXiv 2405.05417; the famous SolidGoldMagikarp is one; hit one and the model outputs garbage). Plus the exit softmax keeps getting more expensive.

You only get 1.75 GiB if you cram in the full 128K, right? Who has inputs that long?

Fair, and exactly the pattern we want them to discover. Drag the console's sequence length to 4K; let them watch it fall. 56 MiB at 4K, 1.75 GiB at 128K, exactly 32×, because it's linear.

Linear is actually the good news. In Step 2 we'll see something that grows with the square, and that's the real trouble. Plant that hook here.

What not to say

The four easiest ways to drift here ① Don't explain tokenization algorithms. BPE merges, vocabulary training: off our thread (doing the math); it eats ten minutes. If asked: “the tokenizer is trained separately; not covered here.”
② Don't explain “why embedding vectors capture meaning.” King minus man plus woman is a nice story, but it's about why the model works, not how to count its resources. This course stays out of the former.
③ Don't say “7,168 has to be divisible by the head count.” (Flagged last segment.) V3 has 128 heads of 128 dims, so attention is 16,384 wide inside, nowhere near 7,168. MLA removed that old constraint.
④ Don't turn “aligned to the hardware” into “so they chose 7168.” Alignment is a hard constraint; width versus depth is a trade-off. 6,144 and 8,192 divide by 128 and 256 just as evenly. Present a constraint as a reason, and any expert stops you with one question.
Pacing Eight minutes, but the real weight is in the last two. One minute on the vocabulary axis (“129,280 isn't special”), five on the lookup and the two costs, the last two on the residual stream as one main road. That's the skeleton of the whole topic. Go slowly.
By the end the room should think: “Only Step 1, and already 3.48 GiB?” If they feel that, Step 2 is easy to teach.

03

Why the KV cache runs away

⏱ 4 min
What this segment must leave behind Multiply two linear terms and you get a disaster. Don't give the answer here. Lay out the math and let the room conclude on its own that “this has to change.”

Script

Scroll to “The weak spot: a runaway KV cache.”

First, why this thing has to exist. Text is generated one token at a time. For every new token, the model looks back at every token before it.

The problem: if you store nothing, every new token recomputes the intermediate results for the tens of thousands before it, from scratch. Recompute for the second token, recompute again for the third. The further you go, the worse the deal.

So the natural fix: store each earlier token's intermediate results and reuse them next time. The two things stored are called K and V; where they live is the KV cache. Nothing wrong with that. It's standard; every model does it.

Stop. Now: “where the trouble is.”

The trouble is how big it gets, and it grows in two directions at once.

Direction one: the longer the sequence, the more you store. A hundred thousand tokens means a hundred thousand copies.
Direction two: every layer stores its own copy. 61 layers means 61 times.

Neither one is scary on its own. But they multiply. When something grows linearly in two directions at once, the product is a disaster.

Point at the log-scale staircase; walk it bar by bar.

But “disaster” is vague. Let's build the number up step by step. Assume for now V3 uses the most traditional attention, with no optimizations.

First bar: one token, one layer. That's 32,768 values to store: K and V, 128 heads, 128 dims per head, multiplied together. In bf16, 64 KiB. Negligible.

Second bar: times 61 layers. One token through the whole model: 3.8 MiB. Still harmless.

Third bar: times 131,072 tokens. 488 GiB.

Stop for two seconds. This segment's first blowup.

Point at the vertical axis: this figure is on a log scale, 10× per gridline. It has to be, or the first bar would be invisible. Left to right spans nearly 8 million times, and in between we did only two very ordinary things: multiply by layers, multiply by tokens.

Point at the red dashed line, then at the number in the red box below.

Let's make that tangible. One TPU v7 device has 94.74 GiB of HBM. This pile needs more than five devices just to hold it. And that's one user, one conversation. Serve a hundred people at once, and multiply by a hundred.

The sharper version is this line. Worth memorizing:

Stored this way, a single conversation of 25,446 tokens fills an entire device with cache alone. And not one byte of model weights has been loaded yet.

Scroll to the flare figure.

Last, how it changes with length. X is sequence length, Y is KV cache, two lines. Red is the traditional scheme; blue is MLA.

Both are straight, because both are linear. But their slopes differ by 56.9×. So they start from the same origin and keep spreading apart. The red gap between them is what a different storage scheme saves.

Say this out loud: it's not a fixed amount. It's a gap that widens with length. The longer the context, the bigger the payoff from storing it differently. That's why, the moment long context arrived, everyone started changing attention.

Segment ends here. Don't go on to how MLA works.

One closing line: “At this point, I don't need to tell you what has to happen next.” Pause, and let them fill it in.

Likely questions

Why not skip the cache and recompute every time? Trade compute for memory.

Possible, but so expensive nobody does it. Without a cache, token n recomputes the n before it, so over a generation total compute goes from linear to quadratic.

And it's the wrong direction: generation is already bound by memory bandwidth, not compute. You'd spend the scarcer resource to save the cheaper one.

Partial recomputation is real; in training it's called recomputation. Different topic; skip it.

If it doesn't fit, can you move it to CPU memory or disk?

Yes: offloading, a mature practice. But it swaps “doesn't fit” for “moves too slowly”: every generated token hauls this pile across once, and bandwidth becomes the new bottleneck.

That's inference systems; this topic does the math, not solutions. Say “that's another route, and the cost is bandwidth,” and move on.

Store the KV in fp8 and you halve it, right?

Right, and widely used. Switch the console to fp8: 488 GiB becomes 244 GiB.

Make clear it's not the same thing as MLA: lowering precision stores each number smaller; MLA stores fewer numbers. The two stack, and real systems often use both.

Lower precision costs quality. How far you can go is an experimental question; this course draws no conclusion.

What about bigger batches? Serving lots of people at once?

One more multiplier, fully linear. Drag the batch slider to 8: the number goes up 8×.

Note the real consequence: the KV cache is one copy per user, while the weights are one copy shared by everyone. So the more people you serve, the bigger the KV cache's share of HBM. At some point it overtakes the weights themselves.

What not to say

The three easiest ways to drift here ① Never reveal how MLA works early. This segment's value is pushing people to the point of “this has to change,” then stopping. Give the answer early and the next segment falls flat.
② Don't get into PagedAttention or memory fragmentation. That's inference-system engineering, not “why is the number so big,” and it won't stop once started.
③ Don't blur the KV cache and the weights together. Keep repeating: the weights are one copy shared by everyone; the KV cache is one copy per user. The Step 7 tally needs it again.
Pacing Four minutes, but the turning point of the whole topic. The first three segments introduce. Here, for the first time, something goes wrong.
One minute per figure. The staircase shows how the number builds; the flare, how it spreads with length. After the closing line, stop. Two seconds of silence beat three more sentences.
04

Three knobs, four generations

⏱ 4 min
What this segment must leave behind The first three generations trade quality for memory. MLA trades compute for memory. The last segment backed the room into “something has to change”; this one shows there are only a handful of places to change. A narrower search space lets the next segment's details land.

Script

Scroll to “Three knobs, four generations.”

We stopped at “I don't need to tell you what comes next.” Let's lay it out, starting with a reassurance: only a few places can change. You can count them.

Split the middle term of the last formula: values per token per layer = 2 × KV heads × head dim. The 2 is one K and one V. Fixed. The other two, plus the layer count, are all the knobs.

Walk the three-row table row by row.

① Copies: the number of KV heads. V3 has 128. The only knob people keep turning, because it's the strongest. To 8: divide by 16. To 1: divide by 128. The cost is as blunt: many Q heads share one K and V, and the lost expressiveness never comes back.

② Width: the head dim. The industry has settled on 128, for two reasons. It sets how much one head can hold; narrow it and every head gets dumber. And kernels are tuned for only a few widths. You save memory, but it runs slowly.

③ Layers: neighboring layers share one copy, or only some layers see the full length. This knob is different. It changes whether you store, not how much, so it's not in the four-generation table. If asked, one sentence.

Stop. Three knobs done; now four generations. Scroll to the four-generation figure. Point at each card, left to right.

The four generations are the history of turning knob one from 128 down to 1.

MHA, 2017. The original Transformer. One KV per head. Best quality, biggest cache.

MQA, 2019. A very short Shazeer paper: “One write-head is all you need.” Every head shares one copy, so you divide straight by the head count.

Say this; it explains the decade that followed. Shazeer wasn't solving “it doesn't fit,” but “it can't move fast enough.” Each generated token rereads K and V from HBM. The bottleneck was bandwidth. “It doesn't fit” came later, with long context.

GQA, 2023. Ainslie et al. Neither one copy nor one per head: shared within groups. It became the default for a practical reason: the paper's recipe converts an existing MHA model to GQA with 5% of the original pre-training compute. No training from scratch.

③ Why RoPE needs its own 64-dim branch

Ask the room: why not apply position encoding to the whole vector? Why carve out 64 dims?

Wait. Most can't answer but get curious: the state you want.

Because otherwise, the cache we just saved gets handed right back.

Inference has an elegant shortcut: fold K's up-projection into Q's matrix ahead of time, so the two become one. Then you never even compute K. Q scores directly against the 512-dim latent in the cache.

But RoPE is a rotation that depends on position, and it would sit between those two matrices.

Matrix multiplication doesn't commute. You can't move what's in the middle, so the fold breaks.

Concretely: for every new token, you'd recompute K for every earlier token. The memory you saved turns into repeated compute.

So they did something clean: they put position on a side path.

Each head's vector splits in two, one job per half. The first 128 dims aren't rotated. They carry content, and they keep the matrices foldable. The last 64 are rotated, and all the position information lives there.

Why does that split work? Because dot products add. The dot product of two concatenated vectors is the front halves' dot product plus the back halves'.

So one 192-dim dot product is content similarity plus positional relevance, computed apart and summed. No interference. Each half keeps its own math.

This position path is only 64 dims, and all 128 heads share one copy.

Check them before class. Get one wrong and someone will do the math. ① q_b_proj is 1,536 × 24,576, not 12,288. 24,576 = 128 heads × 192, and 192 = 128 unrotated + 64 rotated.
② 128 × 128 = 16,384 is the output-side width. It shows up at the input of o_proj (v_head_dim × heads), not at the output of q_b_proj. Easy to mix up.
③ k_pe has 1 head, not 128. So the cache is 512 + 64 = 576, not 512 + 128×64 = 8,704. The whole design hinges on this. Get it wrong and the segment falls apart.

Likely questions

Q is compressed too. Why doesn't that save any cache?

Because the cache holds history, and Q belongs only to the current token.

To generate token N, its Q attends to the K and V of the previous N-1 tokens. K and V must be kept. Q is thrown away after use.

So compressing Q saves weights (smaller matrices) and training activations, a completely separate budget from the inference KV cache. The MLA paper itself says it compresses Q “even if it cannot reduce the KV cache.”

k_pe has one head shared by all 128. Doesn't that limit expressiveness?

There's a loss, but only in the position part, which never needed separate heads.

The 128 content dims are still independent per head: all 128 different filters are intact. What's shared is information like “how far apart are token 5 and token 300,” and that's the same fact for every head.

It's the MQA idea, borrowed locally: the figure's function name, kv_a_proj_with_mqa, gets its “mqa” from this.

If the matrices can be folded, why does the figure still show K and V being computed?

The figure shows the model's math definition, which is also how the public reference implementation computes it.

Absorbing the up-projections into Q and o_proj is an equivalent rewrite inference engines do. It doesn't change the result, only the order of computation.

Stress this: precisely to keep that rewrite possible, RoPE had to be isolated. The architecture made room at design time for an optimization that hadn't happened yet. That's the most intriguing part of this section.

The weights are just a few big matrices. So are the 128 heads real, or only bookkeeping?

Half right. In the projections, it's just bookkeeping. In scoring, heads are physical and can't be merged.

The right half: q_b_proj, kv_b_proj and o_proj are each one block, not 128 small matrices. view means zero data movement and zero compute. “Head i's matrix” is just the i-th column group of the big matrix.

The wrong half, as a counterfactual: with no heads, one dot product of the full 24,576-dim Q and K gives one score per token pair. With 128 heads, each token pair gets 128 scores, each with its own softmax, each weighting its own slice of V.

The results differ completely, and the second can't be one big matmul, because softmax is nonlinear. Linear ops merge in any arrangement. Once a nonlinearity is applied per group, the groups can't be stitched back.

So a head has a lifecycle: at the view it only gets a name, it is truly alive during scoring and softmax, and at o_proj it dies. That big matrix is the only place the 128 heads ever exchange information.

Add a hardware angle (infra people love it): projections are big, regular matmuls, which accelerators like best. Scoring is a batch of small matmuls, with inherently low arithmetic intensity. That's one reason attention has long been a bottleneck.

Why divide by root 192, and not root 128?

Because the dot product runs over 192 dims. The scale follows the dims actually in the dot product, not the content part.

In code it's q_head_dim ** -0.5, with q_head_dim = 128 + 64 = 192.

(V3 also adjusts the factor for YaRN long-context extrapolation. Don't go there. If asked: “long-context extrapolation adds a separate correction; it doesn't change the main line.”)

Are the two matrices just equivalent to one 7,168 × 24,576 matrix?

No, it's a downgrade. Make this clear, or the later “cost” part means nothing.

A single 7,168 by 24,576 matrix can express more transformations. Pinch a 1,536 waist in the middle, and the transformation gets at most 1,536 independent directions. In math terms, its rank is capped.

Analogy: a junction box with 7,168 wires in and 24,576 wires out, but only 1,536 wires in between. Anything can get through, just not as many patterns.

Stricter: there's an RMSNorm in the middle. So it isn't even a product of two matrices; the middle step is nonlinear.

The savings are real: a direct projection takes 176M parameters, the split takes 48.76M, 3.61× fewer. DeepSeek is betting the transformation never needed that much rank anyway.

I can't remember nine shapes. Is there a one-line version?

Yes, and it's the segment's real takeaway:

“Wide in → squeeze to 576 and store → expand into 128 heads on use → back to wide.”

Q follows the same pattern. It just isn't stored. RoPE is the one passenger on this route that gets a lane of its own.

Likely questions

Why sigmoid and not softmax? Isn't softmax more standard?

For sure: softmax forces the 256 scores to sum to 1, a zero-sum constraint. One scoring high pushes the others down, even if all fit. Sigmoid lets each expert judge “does this token suit me?” on its own.

It also pairs more naturally with the bias. A bias on an independent score has a clear meaning (“make it easier to pick”); on a normalized distribution, a muddled one.

(Scope: a design rationale. Not “sigmoid is proven better.” I've seen no controlled comparison.)

If the bias isn't learned, how does it get updated?

By counting actual load and adjusting directly. Too many tokens this batch, nudge its bias down; too few, up.

A feedback controller, not an optimized parameter, so it needs no gradient and never appears in the loss.

Why is the group score “the sum of the top 2 in the group”? Where does the 2 come from?

No guessing: the 2 is derived from a formula the paper gives.

The paper says each token is sent to at most M nodes, chosen by “the sum of the highest Kr/M affinity scores of the experts on each node.” Kr, activated routed experts, is 8; M, the most nodes a token may reach, is 4.

Write on the whiteboard: 8 ÷ 4 = 2.

So “top 2” is just 8 ÷ 4. Allow only 2 nodes, and it becomes “the sum of the top 4.”

And the rule's shape makes sense: at most 4 nodes, 8 experts, so 2 per node on average. Scoring by the top 2 asks, “if I pick this node, what do its best 2 give me?”: exactly the share you'll take from it.

(Scope: the formula is the paper's; the reading “so it matches the share” is ours. The course used to answer “public sources don't explain it; not sure”: not looking hard enough; now fixed.)

Why did V3 switch from V2's softmax to sigmoid?

Certain: the paper states this change explicitly. It says, “Slightly different from DeepSeek-V2, DeepSeek-V3 uses the sigmoid function to compute the affinity scores, and applies a normalization among all selected affinity scores to produce the gating values.”

An easy catch; raise it yourself: if you normalize to 1 at the end anyway, aren't they the same?

No: they act at two different moments. Softmax's denominator covers all 256, baking competition into scoring: raising one pushes down the other 255. Sigmoid has none, so each expert can say on its own, “I'm good at this token.” Competition is deferred to the “rank and take the top 8” step.

(Scope: not “sigmoid is proven better.” V3 published no controlled comparison that I've seen.)

Likely questions

Is “bus” a metaphor, or literally true?

Close to literal, which is why I like it.

That 7,168-dim vector lives in HBM all through the forward pass. Every layer reads it and adds to it: physically the same block of memory, read and written over and over.

Unlike a bus, layers access it strictly in order: no contention. Most accurately: “a bus accessed sequentially.”

If every layer is an optional increment, can you just prune some layers?

Structurally, yes; that's this segment's property. The shape doesn't change, so remove a layer and the rest still run.

But quality drops, by a hard-to-predict amount. What that layer learned isn't backed up elsewhere.

“Can be pruned” isn't “pruning is harmless.” Layer pruning is real, but that's model compression.

What exactly is the difference between pre-norm and post-norm?

Whether the norm touches the trunk.

Pre-norm: normalize, run the sublayer, add its output straight back. The trunk is pure addition, never rescaled.

Post-norm: add, then normalize the whole trunk, so every layer touches that gradient highway once.

With enough layers, post-norm trains unstably. Same as the first reason earlier: no toll booths on the highway.

For something harder: Xiong et al. 2020 (On Layer Normalization in the Transformer Architecture) analyzed gradients at initialization. Post-norm has large gradients near the output layer, so warm-up is required; pre-norm's gradients are well-behaved, so warm-up can be removed entirely.

Note the claim's shape: not “post-norm can't be trained,” but “post-norm adds one more thing to get right, harder to tune with depth.” At tens to hundreds of layers that means “unstable training,” but repeat it precisely.

(Skip norm variants; the course needs one intuition: keep the trunk clean.)

How is 106.75 GiB computed? Why only one copy per layer?

“One copy per layer” is a floor. Real implementations keep other intermediates inside each layer too, so the actual number is only higher.

The floor persuades better: even the leanest accounting doesn't fit.

If asked “how much higher”: about 4.15 TiB, roughly forty times. The bulk is MoE copying each token's activations nine times, and MLA decompressing K and V back to over thirty thousand dims. Do V4's four residual streams make the model four times wider?

No. Be clear, or people hear “four times the parameters.”

The residual stream widens, not each layer's compute width. Sublayers still read and write at 7,168; what changes is how many channels carry data between layers. The V4 paper's own words: “the expanded residual width does not influence the design of the inner layers.”

Numbers you can give: both sizes use nhc = 4 and 20 Sinkhorn-Knopp iterations. V4-Pro has 61 layers and hidden 7,168, identical to V3, so this page's math applies directly. V4-Flash has 43 layers and 4,096.

So does 4× wider mean 4× the activation memory?

The easiest one to get wrong; we've done so ourselves.

“1.75 GiB × 4 = 7.00 GiB per layer” is an upper bound, not the cost actually paid. It assumes all four streams' activations are kept intact at every layer.

The V4 paper is explicit: fused kernels, selective recompute, and a reworked DualPipe overlap. In their words, they “recompute most hidden states between layers and all normalized layer inputs.” A large share is never stored.

So: the paper gives no absolute memory increase; it depends on the recompute setting. The absolute number it does give is for time: mHC's wall-clock overhead is 6.7% of the overlapped 1F1B pipeline stage.

(Always quote 6.7% with its denominator. Not “training is 6.7% slower” but “the overlapped pipeline stage is 6.7% slower.”)

Smaller: you save on those two, but the same text needs more tokens, and compute grows with token count. The attention term grows quadratically.

The compiler is itself a static analyzer. Hand it the model and parallelism config, and compile ahead of time, usually called AOT. It reports the memory needed without allocating a single accelerator.

You know the answer before queuing for hardware: the workflow's cheapest step.

The three points are about how far to trust a tool that answers without running. Not specific to this tool: any performance model or capacity sheet gets the same verdict.

① Compiles ≠ fits

A clean compile means only that the graph is legal. We've seen configs compile all green yet need far more memory than one device has.

Worse: the errors mislead.

Real compile failures often get reported as “out of memory,” so people shrink the batch. Exactly the wrong way.

A wrong error message is more dangerous than none. It sends you confidently the wrong way.

② The number isn't everything

It's only temp buffers. Parameters and optimizer state are a separate ledger.

And it doesn't shrink with parallelism. Shard as wide as you like; temp buffers stay the same size. The easiest one to get wrong: you expect more devices to shrink it, and it doesn't budge.

Far from the limit, AOT is very reliable.

Likely questions

In the peak-activations table, why is it “residual stream ×2”?

Heads-up: “×2” appears three times in that row, for different reasons. Don't lump them. Q/K one each, V/output one each: just two same-size tensors. Only the residual stream's ×2 needs explaining.

A layer first keeps a copy of the incoming x as is, then layer-norms x into a separate buffer. The sublayer computes on that, and the result is added back onto the kept copy.

The add-back is key: because of it, the original x can't be dropped. From the norm until the addition, two 1.75 GiB copies are live at once. That's the ×2.

The MoE layer repeats the same structure, so it's also ×2. The output step has no add-back, so it's one copy, as the table shows. Sharp eyes will spot it; use it as your opening.

13.3 devices: in practice, do you just use 16?

Almost never 14, and not just from rounding.

First, slice topologies have shape constraints. Not every count forms a valid interconnect topology; real sizes land on powers of two or specific length × width × height shapes.

Second, and more important: 14 is a lower bound, not to be taken literally. As the yellow box said, training needs about 105; real planning starts there.

(Skip topology rules; that's Topics 2 and 5. “There are shape constraints” is enough.)

That 9.3 seconds: what do you actually get in practice?

This course hasn't measured it, so no number.

I can say where the gap comes from: devices swap data, synchronize, wait for the slowest one, and recompute to save memory. Together, not a few-percent effect.

That's the value of the clause “assuming perfect, lossless parallelism”: it labels an unknown instead of hiding it.

Attention is 81.8% of compute but only 1.7% of parameters. Isn't that a contradiction?

No contradiction; it's a contrast the course most wants remembered.

Parameter count sets how much you store, regardless of input length. MLA's 11.41B weights are the same whether you feed 4K or 128K.

The attention term in compute grows with the square of sequence length. Long sequences let it eat the compute table while parameters don't budge.

In one line: parameters are static; compute varies with input. Infer one ledger from the other and you'll be wrong; this lecture hit that three times.

105 devices, 14 devices: are those chips or devices?

Devices, not chips: a factor of two.

94.74 GiB is per-device capacity, so anything divided by it counts devices.

A TPU v7 chip holds two devices. So 105 devices is 53 chips; 14 devices is 7 chips. Both right, different things.

What exactly is AOT? What does it need?

It's “compile, don't execute.” The compiler takes the model and parallelism config, lays out the graph and works out the memory, without allocating hardware.

Its value is time, not precision. Devices mean queuing; compiling doesn't. Screen a dozen configs meanwhile.

(Don't teach running AOT here; out of scope. The audience needs only “this exists, and it has three known pitfalls.”)

If it's inaccurate in the critical range, what's it good for?

A lot, used in the right place.

It's good at ruling out, not confirming. Of a dozen candidate configs, it reliably flags the eight that clearly won't work, saving much queuing and trial and error.

It's bad at the final pick between two, which are exactly the ones hugging the limit.

So cut the bulk with it, then run the last two or three on hardware. A sieve, not a judge.

What not to say

Three red lines ① Never turn “13.3” into “so 14 devices can run V3.” It's a lower bound: forward only, batch=1, no activations. Someone will plan with it, off by 8×. Every time, follow at once with “this is the most optimistic value possible.”
② Present the three AOT points as “we hit these,” not general theorems. They're measured experience from our own models and configs. Call them theorems, and the first failed reproduction elsewhere sinks the lecture's credibility.
③ After the 94.74 hook, don't explain it. Even “some is reserved for the system” kills the hook. That opens Topic 2; send people out with the question.

🖥 Screen: open the collapsed “🎯 Warm-up: which collectives per parallelism?” under the Section 0 heading; hold off on “Check answers”.

First, a recap. Topic 5 made five cuts: DP, FSDP, TP, PP, EP. Each cut adds one kind of communication.
Across the top, five kinds: AllReduce, AllGather, ReduceScatter, AllToAll, point-to-point. For each row, tick what a training step needs; more than one is fine. One minute.

💬 Interaction: everyone ticks on the page (or read rows aloud for a show of hands). After a minute, click “Check answers”; ask someone about a red row. Expected: DP: AllReduce; FSDP: AllGather + ReduceScatter; TP: AllReduce; PP: point-to-point; EP: AllToAll.

For DP and TP, AllGather plus ReduceScatter also counts: AllReduce is those two halves joined.
This table is all training, where the answers already exist: compute the whole sequence at once, watch only throughput. Inference differs: someone's at the screen, waiting.

Something you do daily: Paste a four-thousand-token document into a chat box, ask a question, hit Enter.
A short wait, then the first token. Then tokens pop out one by one, steadily. A reasoning model first thinks a long passage to itself.

On the other end, a very expensive chip works for you. Its headline spec: operations per second.
But as those tokens trickle out, that compute mostly sits idle, waiting for data. Two things today: why this is expensive, and how it got cheaper in recent years.

🖥 Screen: stop on Only two waits: first token, then each next. Point at the red dot, then the green boxes.

That scene, as a figure: First the model reads your whole document: prefill. Then it emits the first token: this red dot. Each green box after it is one more token: decode.
As a user you feel just two things: the wait for the first token, and the gaps after.

One term: what the model emits is a token. Today, think of it as roughly a word.
The purple staircase climbs one step per token. That's the KV, coming up: it remembers every earlier token and keeps growing.

🖥 Screen: “The cost: one full haul per token”: the three bullets and the green box, “In one sentence.”

Where's the cost? Our running example: DeepSeek-V3. Each token touches about 37 billion parameters; at one byte each, 37 GB. Why only a slice? Section 1.
Those 37 GB live in HBM: a warehouse. The multiplying happens at a small workbench nearby, about one fifteen-hundredth of the warehouse.

It won't fit, so every token hauls those thirty-odd GB out again.
The second cargo is KV. To write the next token, the model looks back at every earlier one and saves what it found, so the next step reads instead of recomputing.
That's each user's notebook, thicker the longer the chat: at a hundred-odd thousand tokens, over 8 GiB.

Picture a bus. The weights are its own cargo, hauled every trip: fixed fuel, about the same for one rider or a full bus. The notes are each rider's luggage: more riders, more weight.
So only two ways to save: more riders per trip, or a lighter load. Just one leaves the trip alone and splits the two kinds of work: Section 6.

💬 Interaction: ask “More riders, lighter load: which technique comes to mind?” Take two or three. Expected: more people per bus (later: batching) = more riders; smaller numbers (quantization) = lighter load. No answers? The table below answers it.

🖥 Screen: Four years cheaper: each fix leaves a new gap: sweep from 2022 at left, then stop on the seven-row table.

Most savings came step by step along those two roads. Many names; read only the “Bottleneck” column.
In 2022 the bus made no stops: everyone rode to the end with the slowest passenger. Let people on and off at every stop, and it fills up. Once full, notes eat the seats.
Every later row: fix one gap, expose the next. Each section takes one link; don't memorize names today.

Two outside forces kept pushing. Context grew to a million tokens, thickening everyone's notes. Reasoning models think hundreds to thousands of tokens first, so one-at-a-time output became most of the work. They forced several later techniques.

Section 1 · A long prompt in one pass, but output one token at a time? (9′)Planned 9′ · with interaction 6.7′

🖥 Main screen for this segment: course page Section 1 · A long prompt in one pass, but output one token at a time? · main figure One user, one token: 5 ms moving, 0.016 ms computing

🎯 What this segment must leave behind: prefill is compute-bound, decode is bound by data movement; decode carries one rider a trip and still pays full fuel.

Back to Section 0's scene: a four-thousand-token document, a short wait, the first token, then the rest one at a time.
Four thousand tokens read in one pass; output, one pass per token. Same chip; why the difference?

🖥 Screen: “Rows per pass decide: compute- or memory-bound”: the three bullets.

Same bus. Each trip hauls the weights out of HBM once. Each seat holds one token: one row of the matmul.
Prefill is a tour group: the document's ready, all four thousand tokens board at once: a full bus. In decode, each user boards one token per trip: rows on the bus equal users.

Why can't later tokens board early? The second token isn't known until the first arrives.
Training has the answer written, so it computes whole sequences at once. Inference doesn't: one at a time.

So how many riders fill the bus? TPU v7's compute ÷ its bandwidth = 313: the ridge point. In the time to read one byte from HBM, the chip does 313 operations.
Each extra rider adds one operation per byte hauled. Compute stops waiting only at 313 riders. A four-thousand tour group is far past that; one rider uses under a three-hundredth of the compute.

🖥 Screen: the plot under Rows per pass decide: red line, orange dots top right, green dots bottom left.

Orange is prefill: above the line, compute-bound. Green is decode: all below the line, bound by data movement.

💬 Interaction: ask “DeepSeek-V3 reads 37 GB per token; one v7 reads 7.37 TB a second. How long per read?” Wait five seconds. Expected: about five milliseconds. Five seconds? It's TB.

🖥 Screen: One user, one token: 5 ms moving, 0.016 ms computing. Untick “Autoplay”, click “⟲ Reset”, then “Start”: weights load onto the workbench block by block. At the first stop, point at the blue bar, then the near-invisible orange.

Right, five milliseconds. And the compute? 0.016 milliseconds: this orange bar, too thin to see.
A very expensive chip, over 99% of its time waiting on the warehouse.

🖥 Screen: click “Next”: the second token hauls the full load again.

Next token? The workbench can't hold 37 GB; that load is gone, so haul it all again. Another five milliseconds, one token.

🖥 Screen: “Next” again: 313 rows a trip; orange catches up with blue.

Same load, 313 riders. Only now does compute catch up with data movement.
Flip it, more interesting: below that, one more rider barely lengthens the trip. The bus runs anyway; extra riders ride free.
So faster compute won't help. Bandwidth sets the floor.

🖥 Screen: More users: weights amortize, KV doesn't: blue segment, then purple.

The fix: more riders per trip. The bus's cargo is shared, so each rider's blue segment shrinks.
But purple doesn't move: each user reads their own notes; no pooling spreads that.

As chats get long, purple takes over. A few hundred riders, each with a hundred-odd-thousand-token chat: notes are nearly 80% of the load.
In the end, notes fill the seats: Section 3.

🖥 Screen: MoE breaks the batch apart again: filled square left, then thin line right.

One more cut. Each layer has two steps: attention, which looks back through the notes, then a big feed-forward network. MoE splits it into many pieces: experts.
In bus terms, each layer is a transfer station. DeepSeek-V3 has 256 branch lines per station; each token rides only eight. That's the earlier “each token touches only 37 billion.”

💬 Interaction: ask “You finally got 256 people aboard. At the transfer, each picks eight branch lines. How many ride each line, on average?” Wait five seconds. Expected: eight (256 × 8 ÷ 256).

Right, just eight. Each branch line is its own bus, hauling its own weights for eight riders. Empty again: back to a few riders a trip.
To fill every line, by the full edition's H100 math, a step needs over nine thousand tokens. Section 4 solves it: spread the experts over more devices.

🖥 Screen: the green box, “Takeaway.”

Take away one line: prefill is compute-bound, decode is bound by data movement. To earn back each trip's fuel, carry more riders.
The next three sections take that road: keep the bus full, free seats from the notes, regather the riders MoE scattered. Step one: why not just add more users?

Section 2 · Just add users? How big can the batch get? (9′)Planned 9′ · with interaction 7.4′

🖥 Main screen for this segment: course page Section 2 · Just add users? How big can the batch get? · main figure Gray cells pile up…

🎯 What this segment must leave behind: continuous batching keeps the seats full; KV sets how many; more riders, slower each.

Last section: one rider per decode trip is a waste, so carry more. This trick changes no model code or kernels, only the queue, and serves tens of times more users.
What's hard? Riders don't board or leave together. One asks “what day is it?” and goes. Another wants a 3,000-word report.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Old way”; stop on its two bullets.

The earliest way is static batching: fill the bus, leave and arrive together. Early finishers can't get off; their seats ride empty.
Every step still hauls the weights once; empty seats ride for nothing.
Why not swap riders midway? Not for lack of ideas. Old engines had one button, run the whole batch; the scheduler never saw which step it was on.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Stop at every step”, play Gray cells pile up…; point at the gray cells growing on top, then below.

Then Orca, in 2022. First idea: after every step, stop and look; whoever's done gets off at once, whoever's at the door gets on.
Watch: gray cells, empty seats, pile up on top; below, none. Same steps, the bottom finishes clearly more.

This became continuous batching. vLLM and SGLang both do it today.
Same bus: the old way is a charter: fill it, arrive together. Continuous batching is a real bus: every stop, some off, some on.

But riders are at different points: one on token three, another on token 3,000. How can they share a trip? Orca's second idea.
The weight matmul doesn't care whose row it is. Line everyone up in one strip and multiply; the weights are read once, shared.

Only attention can't merge. It means rereading your own notes, and everyone's differ in length and content, so each reads their own.
Most of a step is merged; only note-reading runs separately.

Boarding needs care too. Swallow a prompt of tens of thousands of tokens at once, and that step runs long, stalling everyone mid-output.
So cut it into chunks, one per step; the rider boards gradually. That's chunked prefill. After the last chunk, the first token comes out at once; next step, they're a regular rider.

One or two board per step. So how does the bus reach hundreds? Boarding takes one step; emitting keeps you aboard hundreds or thousands. Add a rider every few steps and it stays full.
The door isn't the bottleneck. The seats are.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Seats are KV”; stop on the first paragraph. Don't scroll on: the figure below answers question two.

How many can ride at most? Each rider takes a block of KV: last section's notebook. As many notebooks as memory holds, that many seats.
One equation links seats and throughput: tokens per second is roughly riders over the time between tokens.

💬 Interaction: ask “At a steady 50 ms between tokens, how many riders does one device need to emit 10,000 tokens a second?” Pause five seconds. Reveal: 500 riders (10,000 × 0.05 s).

What if memory holds only 300 notebooks? Throughput is pinned around 6,000. Everyone else at the door just queues.
That's running out of seats.

💬 Interaction: ask “So add seats. On GB300 with V4-Pro, a bit more KV-pool memory made room for 13% more riders. By the formula, how much should throughput rise? How much did it?” Pause five seconds; most say 13. Reveal: only 0.8%.

🖥 Screen: down to Over 10% more seats…: top and bottom bars, then the middle two.

The formula's fine. Our mistake: assuming the time between tokens stays put. The extra riders cost everyone: the gap grew almost 10%.
Why? The weights were already shared; a newcomer brings only their own notes. More riders, more notes to read, slower steps.
A bit over 10% more riders, almost 10% slower steps. Divide, and it nearly cancels.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “So batch size”; stop on Throughput vs. interactivity…, trace the green line right to left.

The whole tradeoff, drawn, is this line. X-axis: tokens per second per user; y-axis: total tokens per second per GPU.
Move left: a fuller bus, a better-paid GPU, slower users. Cut each user's speed by more than half, and each GPU emits ten times the tokens.
So fix “how fast per user” first. From here on, that's the interactivity tier.

When someone says “our per-GPU throughput is double yours,” ask first: how fast per user? They may just be further left on the line.
Production does pick left: DeepSeek gives each user only twenty-odd tokens a second, for high throughput.

🖥 Screen: the green “Takeaway” box.

Three steps, then. Carry more: continuous batching keeps seats full. How many at most: as many notebooks as fit in memory. Carry too many: each rider slows a bit.
What's the trick worth? In the Orca paper, at the same latency, it served 36.9 times the old way's requests.

Notebooks set the seats, so naturally: how thick is each one, and can it be thinner?
A thinner notebook wins twice: more riders fit, and less to read each step makes everyone faster. This section was all tradeoffs; this is a rare free win.

Section 3 · KV cache: how big, how to shrink? (8′)Planned 8′ · with interaction 6.4′

🖥 Main screen for this segment: course page Section 3 · KV cache: how big, how to shrink? · main figure 8× the context…

🎯 What this segment must leave behind: seats = KV pool ÷ notebook thickness. To seat more: thinner lines, then no wasted paper, then no redoing.

Last section ended here: seats are notebooks. Every token gets a line, and writing the next one means rereading every line before it.
How thick is it? Work it out the oldest way first. It'll shock you.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Step one: same model shape, line thickness differs over 50×”; stop on the three bullets.

The model reads its notes from many angles at once. One watches grammar; another tracks who “he” is. Each angle is an attention head.
The oldest scheme, MHA, gives every head its own copy. At V3's shape, one user filling the context needs over 500 GB of notes alone; two and a half chips couldn't hold it. That's our derivation; stored that way for real, it won't run.

So step one: thinner lines. GQA has several heads share a copy: Llama's eight groups mean only eight copies. DeepSeek's MLA goes further: each layer stores one compressed summary, and every head computes against it.
For the notes, MLA counts as one KV head; we'll need that next section. Divide, and the notes shrink to about 1/57 of MHA. That division is ours.

Does thinner make the model dumber? The GQA paper tested one model: decoding over five times faster, average score barely moved.
One model, one machine. Trust the direction; don't copy the multiplier.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Thinner isn't enough: longer notes, fewer riders”; stop on 8× the context…. Point at the three-segment bar, then the green line.

But thinner lines still grow in number with every token. Look: half a v7 chip, which software calls one device. Minus weights and a small reserve, forty-odd GiB is left for notes. Green is MLA: at 4K context, over 170 riders fit.

💬 Interaction: ask “Same device, same V3, context from 4K to 32K, eight times longer. How many riders?” Pause five seconds. Reveal: exactly one eighth, 172 → 21. Then the red X: MHA seats no one before 32K.

Double the tokens, halve the riders. So long context costs twice: fewer riders share each trip, and each reads thicker notes every step.
That's it for thickness. What's left is how the paper is laid out.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Step two: no wasted paper, a page at a time”; play Request 2 leaves…. When request 2 ends, point at blocks 2 and 5.

Step two: don't waste paper. The old way couldn't know who'd write how much, so it reserved everyone the thickest notebook, in one contiguous chunk. Unwritten space sits empty; no one can borrow it.

🖥 Screen: scroll to “Accuracy: error crosses four gates…”; point at the four gates. At “how much you lose”, down to Same 4 bits…: red dot (right), gray (left), green (middle), then the two lines below.

Follow one error on its trip: from a rounded weight to a token coming out, it crosses four gates.
Gate one: land somewhere flat. Training walks downhill toward lower error, so a trained model sits on a valley floor, mostly flat. Nudge a weight a little, and most directions don't hurt. GPTQ pushes the error toward the flat directions.
Gate two: errors cancel. Rounding goes up about as often as down; add a few thousand values and most of it cancels. The danger is that long steel pipe, hence small groups.

Gate three: only first place counts. Small noise jiggles every word's score; the winner changes only if the top two were already close.
Gate four is the amplifier: softmax. People often say softmax recovers the error; it's the opposite. A small score difference becomes an exponential probability difference. Expert outputs are added straight back, so their error stays linear. Attention scores and expert routing both pass through softmax, so protect them. One paper took attention computation straight to 8-bit integers: Llama2 fell to about chance on a multiple-choice test. Smoothing outliers first, the same paper got 8 bits nearly lossless.
So everyone keeps attention at 8 bits at most, with special handling.

Absorbed error doesn't mean no drop: like any medicine, quantization has side effects. At the same 4 bits, the loss depends on how you quantize. Averages lie too: accuracy can stay flat while right and wrong answers swap and cancel.
The figure shows perplexity: roughly, how many words the model hesitates between per token; lower is better. Red, plain rounding, sits far from gray, 16-bit. Green picks its ruler more carefully and wins back half the gap.

One step further: a format designed for 4 bits. Per NVIDIA's blog, R1 from 8 bits to NVFP4 lost under 1% on main language tasks.
Furthest of all: train for 4 bits from the start. Kimi K2 Thinking officially says “lossless,” naming no baseline precision. So when you hear “lossless,” ask: compared with what?

Section 1 · One number → 671B derivatives: backpropagation (22′)

🎯 Keep two: why compute is 3×; the pivot “backward needs forward values.”

⛔⛔ The lecture's only theory segment; easy to botch. It fails one way: opening with the chain rule. ⭐ Don't. First let the room feel how dumb the dumb method is.

A dumb question first: which way do I turn one parameter?
The direct way: nudge it, rerun the whole network, see how far the loss moves.
…With 671B parameters: 671B forward passes.
At a second each: almost twenty thousand years. For one step.

⭐⭐⭐ The foundation. Pause after:

So backpropagation's real problem is not “how to take a derivative.” That's high-school math.
It's squeezing 671B forward passes into one.

⭐⭐⭐ But first fill one gap; it decides who keeps up. ⛔ At “high-school math,” the room splits: half nod, half think “I never got it.” ⭐ All that follows rests on three words: derivative, partial derivative, chain rule. Three minutes here, and the rest goes smoothly. No limits, no formulas. One phrase: exchange rate.

🖥 Screen: Derivative, partial, chain rule, Ⓐ only.

A parameter is a shower knob.
The reading on the right is the loss: how many degrees the water is off.
I turn the knob a tiny bit toward hot, by 0.01.
Reading: 4.00 to 4.03. It moved 0.03.
⭐ So the knob's derivative is 0.03 over 0.01: 3.
Read it as: you move one notch, it moves three.

⛔⛔ Stress this: the easiest thing to mix up.

Careful: the derivative is not “the reading is 4.00.”
4.00 is a value. 3 is an exchange rate. Different things.
⭐ When this lecture says “this gradient is big,” it means the rate is big, not the parameter.

⭐⭐ While “exchange rate” is fresh, thirty seconds on where the word comes from. ⛔ Asked in class: “Isn't derivative the same as deviation?” A mix-up; correct it on the spot: deviation means “offset” (standard deviation). ⭐ Answer properly: the word itself explains the idea.

On the word: it's derivative, not deviation (“offset”).
⭐ Derive comes from the Latin dērīvāre: de, “from,” and rivus, “stream.”
Literally: draw a stream of water off a river.

So the name fits: derive = draw something off.
Today “derive” just means “work out”; the water picture is lost.
⭐⭐ Back to the knob: no new river; a second stream off the same one.
Stream one: “this input gives this output.”
Stream two: “move one notch, how many does it move.” Same source.

🖥 Screen: pan to Ⓑ. ⭐ Point at the row of knobs; hand on the screen.

What's “partial” in “partial derivative”? Three words: hold the rest.
Partial means “part”: one variable at a time.
This row: I move only the middle knob and hold the others still.
The rate measured that way is the partial derivative.
⭐⭐⭐ This shower has 671B knobs.
Stack each knob's number in one column: that column is the “gradient.”
⛔ Remember: the gradient isn't one number. It's 671B numbers.

🖥 Screen: pan to Ⓒ.

Last word: the chain rule.
The catch: the knob isn't wired straight to the temperature; stages sit between.
Stage one: knob moves one notch, A moves two. Rate 2.
Stage two: A moves one, B moves half. Rate 0.5.
Stage three: B moves one, the gap moves three. Rate 3.
⭐ Total rate, knob to gap? 2 times 0.5 times 3: three.

⭐⭐ Add two sentences at once, or the three numbers sound made up. ⛔ The room got stuck here last time.

⭐ See the little orange box between boxes? That's the actual device: a block of weights plus an activation.
The number above isn't the device. It's its slope,
weight times the activation's slope at this point.
⛔ So the numbers aren't made up. Each device computes its own.

⭐⭐⭐ Always say this, so nobody takes the figure literally.

⛔ Precisely: this is a 1-D toy. One knob, one reading.
A real stage is 7,000-odd dimensions in, 7,000-odd out.
⭐⭐ Its “slope”? Not one number, a whole table: each output dimension's rate against each input dimension.
⛔ But no such square matrix sits in the machine; no weight block is 7,168 on both sides. The table is only a concept.
⭐ Good news: we never compute it. The backward pass just multiplies the blame by it, giving the upstream stage's blame.
So “multiply all the way” is true; you just multiply tables.

⭐⭐⭐ The analogy. After it, “chain rule” never sounds scary.

You do this all the time: currency exchange.
Dollars to euros is one rate, euros to yen another. Dollars to yen? Multiply them.
⭐⭐ That's all the chain rule is.
⛔ The interesting part: which end do you start multiplying from?
Result: exactly the same. Cost: twenty thousand years apart.
Coming up.

⭐ Close the gap with one line:

The rest of this lecture needs only three sentences:
Derivative: move one notch, how many it moves. Partial derivative: the same, everything else held still. Chain rule: multiply the stages between.
No limits, no ε, no formulas to memorize.

⛔⛔ Before “the loss is one number,” a minute to fence it in. Skip it, and all of 1.5 trips here. ⭐ Pushback from class: “One token has over 120,000 numbers, one sequence over 4,000 positions, times batch. How is that one?”

To be clear: all those numbers exist. They're just not all the loss; two compressions sit between.
⭐ First: at one position, each of the 129,000-odd vocabulary tokens gets a score. That's “the model's answer,” not “how bad it was.”
We take only the correct slot's probability, negative log. 129,000 becomes one.

🖥 Screen: One loss per token, Ⓐ: point at the grid.

⭐ Second: 4,000-odd positions per sequence, each with its own loss; times batch, tens of millions per step.
Add them all, divide by the token count.
⭐⭐ Tens of millions become one. That's the point on the loss curve.

⭐⭐⭐ Why it must be compressed: the point of this gap.

Why insist on one number? Not for looks.
⭐⭐⭐ One number at the output is what makes one backward pass possible.
In 1.5 the seed goes in at the right end, one number, so you plant it once.
⛔ With 4,000-odd objectives, you'd plant it 4,000-odd times.
Hence multi-objective optimization is expensive.

⭐ Last, defuse an apparent contradiction. Fifteen seconds.

“the loss is one number” and “the backward seed is over half a billion numbers” are both true.
⭐ The loss itself is a scalar; its derivative is every position × the whole vocabulary: over half a billion numbers.
A quantity vs. its derivative.

🖥 Screen: From logits to loss, Ⓐ only.

This row is the logits.
⛔ Positive or negative, no range, not probabilities yet.
Three steps to the loss.

⭐ One, exponentiate: all positive, gaps stretched.
⭐ Two, divide by the sum: they add up to one. Now they're probabilities.
⭐ Three, take the correct answer's probability, negative log.
On screen the correct slot is 0.498, so the loss is 0.696. Done.

⭐ Then Ⓑ: one minute, thirty seconds per property.

🖥 Screen: pan to Ⓑ.

Two properties: one elegant, one that saves you.
⭐ One: add the same number to every logit; probabilities don't change. Only differences matter; the exponential turns differences into ratios.
⛔ Two: don't exponentiate directly; it blows up. Real logits reach tens or hundreds; one exponential overflows fp32.
⭐ So implementations subtract the row's max first, using that shift invariance. Mathematically equal, numerically safe.

⭐⭐⭐ Then pose Ⓒ's question. Don't answer yet; let the room find it. ⛔ In class people found it themselves: far better than being told.

Look at that expression again: minus ln p.
❓ It holds only the correct slot's probability.
The other 129,000-odd tokens should be zero but aren't yet. So why does none show up in the loss?

⭐⭐⭐ Wait three seconds, then answer: the point of the part.

🖥 Screen: pan to Ⓒ.

⭐⭐ They do take part, just not by name: all in the denominator.
The correct answer's probability is its exponential over the sum of all.
That sum covers the whole vocabulary.

Example. Left, original: loss 0.696.
Right: I only raise one “wrong” score by one notch. ⛔ The correct slot is untouched.
⭐⭐⭐ The loss rises to 0.970.

And take the derivative: they all show up:
correct −0.50; the other four +0.18, +0.18, +0.07, +0.07.
⭐ Each wrong one's gradient is exactly the probability it holds: hold more, get pushed down harder. They sum to zero.
⭐⭐ Remember: invisible in the loss, visible in the gradient. And training uses only the gradient.

📌 If asked “Doesn't cross-entropy sum over the whole vocabulary?”: Yes, the full form is a sum; with a one-hot label only one term is nonzero. “Looking at one” follows from one-hot; it isn't the definition. ⭐ With soft labels (label smoothing, distillation), every term really appears in the loss.

⭐⭐ If asked “Why does the loss go from 11-something to 2?”, give the mapping, then always add the part below. ⛔ The lecture keeps saying “near 2, every 0.01 is expensive” but never says why.

First: not “similar”; two faces of one number.
⭐ Perplexity = e to the loss, meaning “how many tokens the model is hesitating between.”
Top, 11.77: random guessing over the whole vocabulary. Bottom, 2: hesitating between seven or eight.
⛔ Precisely: the top is set by vocabulary size; another vocabulary, another number.

⭐ That's the seed: “prediction minus truth” in plain words.

We can't change the output. Now what?
To raise a number, only three ways: change the bias, the weights, or brighten the previous layer.
⭐ The third is a dead end: we can't change that layer either, so the request passes back one layer. That's all “backpropagation” means.

Here the blame is the same; only the first factor, brightness, differs.

🎯 Keep one criterion (FLOPs per byte saved) and one story (same criterion, flipped conclusion).

The last section left us owing a full chain of activations, first layer to last.

It's also this course's first real trade-off: not “is there a better way?” but you get one of two. Which?

🖥 Screen: Full recompute: 1/3 more compute…, only the two bar groups in Ⓐ. ⭐ Shape, not numbers. Let the room see one bar grow a sliver, the other nearly vanish.

Recomputation in one line: save checkpoints, not the work.

Cost: one extra forward pass, compute from 3× to 4×. A third more.
⭐ In return, that number shrinks to a twentieth.

⛔ 2026-09-22: this spot used to repeat the two numbers that had just closed the last section. Mere tallying. This cell carries the exchange rate, not the numbers.

⭐ That exchange rate is so lopsided it barely counts as a trade-off.

⭐⭐ The core: the criterion. Explain it fully; cut later material first.

How to choose? One number.
Ask each intermediate tensor: drop it and recompute it in backward, and how many FLOPs do I pay per byte saved?
Low ratio, drop it; high ratio, keep it. The whole ranking falls out of that.

For a linear layer it has a surprisingly clean form: exactly the matmul's input width.

🎯 Keep one: judge any stability fix by both “did it stabilize?” and “did quality drop?”

⭐⭐ This section is special: the only one on “something went wrong, now what?” Sections 1–5 did accounting, which can't answer the question asked most on a real run. (⛔ Fixed 2026-09-22: it said “the previous six sections”.)

Accounting done.
But on a real machine, what you hear most isn't “out of memory,” but: “Uh oh. The loss just shot up.”

⭐⭐⭐ One minute naming them first, or the whole section is just “bad.” ⛔ New block (page 6.0). ⭐ Nothing new: it lines up five failure modes from five sections, never before named apart.

First, a roll call. This lecture has covered five ways training goes bad, under five names. On a real run, the hard part isn't the fix. It's knowing which you have.
⭐ By what you see on screen:
One: the loss goes NaN. Exploding gradients.
Two: the early layers never move. Vanishing gradients.
⭐ These two are one chain of products, going two ways: factors above one on average, it grows exponentially; below one, it collapses. So the fixes are one family too.

Five: a sudden spike in smooth training. That's today's spike.

⛔⛔ Always say the three-and-four line: the table's most valuable row.

⛔ Three and four both get called “it won't train,” but need opposite fixes.

Mix them up: every fix backfires.

First instinct? That batch of data must be bad.
They tested it cleanly: fed the batches around the spike again, from an earlier checkpoint.

⭐ So a spike isn't caused by bad data.

Now a framework for fixes. Three kinds, at three places.
Upstream, fix the structure, hard to blow up: norm inside the residual block, QK-norm, initialization.
Midstream, fix the numerics, capping what grows too large: z-loss, gradient clipping.
Downstream, salvage: roll back, skip data.
⭐ Upstream is nearer a cure; downstream is first aid. Go in order.

⭐⭐⭐ Go deep on one: the structural fix. It also settles 3.5's open question. ⛔ Moved here 2026-09-20 (round 20) from the section start, before “three kinds,” where “fix two” came before the count. Numbering is a promise: list first.

🖥 Screen: In Post-LN, the norm itself is a culprit.

⭐⭐ Parts first, then placement. No numbers here.

First take one layer apart, or the difference has nowhere to land.
A layer is two paths side by side.
The trunk: the input goes straight through, untouched.
The branch: the same input goes through attention or the MLP to compute a correction.
At the end they merge: Section 1's residual.
⭐ Normalization is the third part. It must sit somewhere on those paths.

The two placements differ only in where it goes.
The old way: merge first, then run the norm over the whole trunk. The trunk is touched every layer.
The new way: the norm is tucked inside the branch. Nobody touches the trunk; it runs straight to the top.
Hold that picture. You can't yet tell which is better.

Now the counterintuitive part: in the old placement, the norm itself is a culprit behind vanishing gradients.
⭐ Strange: we thought normalization was there to save us.

⭐ Mechanism in plain words. No derivation. ⛔ Say “variance one plus one is two” and the room follows arithmetic instead. Numbers are in 6.5.

In one sentence: the old way adds trunk and branch, then normalizes.
At initialization they are two unrelated random vectors. Add them, and the energy doubles.
The norm pulls the energy back down.
⭐ So every layer squeezes the signal down a notch.
By the deep layers, only a trace of the input is left.
The red line: the residual “shortcut” is cut down exponentially, hardest near the input.

⭐⭐ The pointed question. Three seconds.

So: if it's that bad, why not remove the norm?

Without it, every residual pushes the energy up a notch, until it's out of control.
⭐ So the question was never “whether to add a norm.” It's “where.”
The new way: normalize, then feed the branch; the trunk runs straight through.
Every residual branch now carries equal weight; the exponential decay is gone.

⭐⭐⭐ The high point; the rest was setup. Say it alone, pause. ⭐ On screen, both halves: identical curves, same axes.

The most counterintuitive part:
in a different setting, this “bug” becomes a feature.

What do we want when we finetune?
Adjust parameters near the output; barely move those near the input, so we keep what pretraining learned.
“Vanishing gradients” is just that: the nearer the input, the less it moves the output.
⭐⭐ Exactly what finetuning wants.
So an old-placement model often finetunes better than a new-placement one.
Same curve: a bug in pretraining, a feature in finetuning.

⭐ Close Section 3's warmup question. Two sentences; no proof.

This also answers Section 3's open question: why warmup exists.
In the old placement, gradients near the output are large right at initialization. A large learning rate then is bound to be unstable; warmup holds it back.
In the new placement, gradients behave from the start, so you can drop warmup.
⭐ So Section 3's three explanations don't compete.
One says why it was once indispensable, another why it's still around. Different questions.

⭐ But don't stop at “you can drop it.” V3 uses the new placement and still warms up.
The reason changed:
then to survive; today to allow a higher peak, which speeds up the rest of training.

⭐ A one-minute story, same pattern as 6.1's scale effect.

Another structural fix, with a story.
At billions of parameters, vision Transformers diverged within a few thousand steps.
The cause: attention scores ran away; after the softmax the weights collapsed onto almost one position.
⭐ It stopped “distributing attention” and stared at one spot.
The fix: normalize Q and K before the dot product, the QK-norm many large models now use by default.
⚠️ Attribution: that paper didn't invent it; it says “we adopt the approach of” others. We cite it because it applied it at twenty-two billion parameters, with a comparison.
⛔ Again: a failure seen only at scale.

⭐⭐ Last, two misconceptions. The first gets a figure with one real number line.

🖥 Screen: fp16's problem isn't “too coarse”; it's “can't go low”.

‘fp16 needs loss scaling, so bf16 must need it too.’ No.
⛔ But not because ‘sixteen bits is enough’: fp16 is sixteen bits too, and finer than bf16.
⭐ Same number of slots, split differently.
fp16 spends more on fine resolution, bf16 on reaching very small values.

⭐⭐ fp16's real problem isn't coarseness. It can't go low enough.
A small enough gradient becomes exactly zero; that parameter gets no signal that step.
On this log axis: fp16's zero line is here, and bf16's floor is far to the left. The green span between: everything bf16 can store and fp16 can't.

So what does loss scaling do? Simple: lifts values off the floor.
Before backprop, scale the loss up by a big factor, lifting values about to fall through. Before the update, scale back down.
⭐ bf16's floor is too far to hit; no need.
⚠️ Its cost is elsewhere: it's coarse. bf16 fears accumulating tiny increments, not falling to zero: Section 3's criterion again.

⭐⭐ The second: no figure, one minute. Without it, 6.2's rescue hangs in the air.

Second: is training just impossible to reproduce?
⭐ No. It can be, and it's worth it. PaLM achieved bitwise reproducibility: restart from any checkpoint, and every later step matches the original, to the bit.

Two things. One: the compute framework is deterministic.
⭐ Two, easily missed: the data pipeline must be deterministic too.
The trick: the batch depends only on the step number, not on device count or restarts.

❓ Why does this matter?
Recall the rescue: on a blowup, roll back, skip those batches, keep going.
⭐⭐ That fix depends on exactly this.
With a nondeterministic pipeline you couldn't even say which batches to skip: you wouldn't know what you just fed in.

We quoted a lot of numbers today. What I want you to keep is four criteria.

One: whether you need high precision comes down to two things: how far this quantity moves per step, versus the smallest step the format resolves.
⭐ Under one step, you haven't moved.
Moving averages forget a little every step, so they can move. Master weights build up over a hundred thousand steps, a few thousandths per step, so they can't.

Two: whether to recompute depends on how much compute you pay for each byte you save.
For a linear layer it's fixed: the input width. For attention it grows with the sequence.
⭐ Two different slopes. So they have to cross.

Three: if two quantities grow at different rates in the same variable, and the faster-growing one starts lower, their ranking will flip sooner or later.
⛔ You can't drop “starts lower.” If the faster one also starts higher, the lines never cross.
⭐ So any “A is cheaper than B” must come with the range where it holds.
In this lecture it flipped several times, each in a different variable.

Four is the one I just gave: to judge any trick that stabilizes training, check both “did it stabilize?” and “did quality drop?”

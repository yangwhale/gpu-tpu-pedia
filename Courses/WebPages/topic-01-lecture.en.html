00

Fix the ruler: how we do the math

⏱ 3 min
What this segment must leave behind Fix the ruler first, then measure, and never swap it midway. Students must accept: no conclusions here, only math, with the assumptions nailed down first.

Script

Stay on the first screen. Don't scroll yet.

First, the method, because it's not how most material on large models works.

Most material hands you conclusions: MoE saves compute, MLA saves memory, FlashAttention is faster. All true, but heard. And with things you heard, change the setting and you can't tell if they still hold.

This lecture does one thing: the math.

How? Take a real model, DeepSeek V3, and follow one token from input to output. At every component, ask the same four questions on the spot: how big are the weights, the activations, how many multiplications? And the fourth, the one that matters most: does anything blow up here?

You'll find that last question is usually the point.

One beat. The real content of this segment starts here.

But measuring needs a ruler. The usual mistake isn't bad arithmetic; it's switching rulers halfway. So we nail the assumptions down now, for good.

Scroll to the four “Assumptions” cards.

Five. One: sequence length 128K, i.e. 131,072 tokens, roughly a short book. Two: batch of one; get one sequence right, multiply later. Three: bf16 precision, two bytes per number. Four: forward only, no backward. Five: the yardstick is one TPU v7 device, 94.74 GiB; every number we compute gets divided by it, or you have no feel for it.

Number four needs explaining. Why forward only?

Optionally ask “Anyone want to guess why?”, then answer yourself.

Because forward is fact; backward is mixed with decisions.

Forward is matmul after matmul, each step a fixed size. No choice. Backward: first decide. Keep intermediates or recompute them? Which device holds optimizer state? Accumulate gradients over steps? Strategies. Choose differently, get completely different numbers.

Mix decisions into facts and the math gets muddy. So we count only the part everyone pays. Backward gets Topic 4, when we know the math and strategy is worth discussing.

If the room is impatient, jump to the “First, guess a number” quiz, then come back for the assumptions.

OK, the ruler's set. Now, Step 1: how a word turns into a row of numbers.

Likely questions

Why 128K? Why not 4K or 1M?

Because 128K is today's norm, and it sits right at the tipping point. At 4K problems hide: attention takes only about 10% of compute (computed later). At 128K, about 80%: the bottleneck has moved.

At the key moment, drag the slider at the top in front of them.

Batch of 1? Isn't that unrealistic?

On purpose. Batch is a multiplier: it scales every term equally and only hides the proportions.

Batch=1 shows the structure. For realism, move the slider at the top to 8.

What's bf16? Why not fp32 or fp8?

bf16 is today's default precision for training and inference: two bytes per number. fp32 is four bytes, more precise but twice the cost. fp8 is one byte, cheaper but numerically risky.

Stop there. Precision is Topic 8; “two bytes per number” is all the math needs.

Where does 94.74 come from?

It's the usable HBM of one TPU v7 device: a hardware constant, not something we measured.

If pressed on what's inside the device: that's Topic 2. Note it and move on, or it eats your time.

Where does 576 come from? Why not 512?

512 is the compressed KV, 64 the RoPE slice: together, what one token stores per layer. In the config: kv_lora_rank = 512 and qk_rope_head_dim = 64.

Why not compress the 64 RoPE dims too? One line, “they carry position; compress them and they're useless,” then stop. Step 2 covers this asymmetry; expanding now blows the time.

MQA needs only 3.81 GiB, even less than MLA. Why not just use MQA?

The best question you'll get, and the crux of Step 2. Don't fully answer yet.

Say only: MQA saves by making all heads share one K/V copy, and the lost expressiveness is gone for good. MLA saves almost as much without any head paying. Step 2 is the how.

Then park it on the board; point back at MLA. In the opening, an open hook beats an answered question.

So how much does backward cost? Can you give a rough number now?

A directional answer settles the room: compute roughly triples, and the biggest memory chunk is optimizer state, larger than the model itself.

Then stop: the line signals “more math is coming,” not Topic 4.

What not to say

The three easiest ways to drift here ① Don't start explaining what a Transformer is. Newcomers pick it up along the “follow the token” thread; a crash course takes ten minutes and wrecks the pace.
② Don't explain bf16's bit layout. Say “two bytes” and move on.
③ Don't linger on “why 94.74.” That's Topic 2.
Pacing Three minutes, max. It's the foundation, not the house. What grabs them is the Step 1 moment: “we haven't entered layer 1, and a chunk of HBM is already gone.” Get there early.

The model card: reading the table

⏱ 2 min
What this segment must leave behind Every number here comes from the officially published config file. None is estimated, none measured by us. It decides whether the room believes your later math. Say it out loud.

Script

Scroll to the three “The model card” tables.

The table has over twenty rows. Don't read them one by one. People tune out and won't remember them anyway.

Pull out just four numbers. Point back to the rest as they come up.

Click each of the next four on the table as you say it.

First: hidden_size is 7168. The width of the residual stream, the main road through the whole model. Every activation later is token count times this number. If the room remembers one number, make it this one.

Second: 61 layers. Nothing unusual. The next row is: first_k_dense_replace is 3, so the first 3 layers are ordinary MLPs and MoE starts at layer 4. Precisely: “3 dense layers plus 58 MoE layers.” We'll reuse that 3 and 58 when counting parameters.

The third number is actually a false: tie_word_embeddings = false. The big lookup matrix at the entrance and the exit matrix that turns vectors back into words don't share weights. Two separate copies. Many models share to save a copy. V3 doesn't, and pays over 900 million extra parameters.

Plant a hook here. Don't answer it.

Ask in passing: “Why do some models share and some don't?” Then: “We'll get to that at the exit, the last step.” Not now.

The fourth isn't a number but a sentence, the most important one:

Every number in this table comes from DeepSeek's officially published config file. None is estimated, and none is our own measurement. Every later derivation starts from here.

Say it: the final tally gives 671 billion total parameters and 36.6 billion active, matching the official figures. That's when this sentence pays off.

Likely questions

How was the width 7168 chosen?

Two hard factors: align with the hardware's matrix units (7168 is a multiple of 128), and balance against layer count: same parameter budget, wider or deeper? Each model redoes it; no standard answer.

⚠️ Don't say “7168 must divide by the head count.” Not true for V3. 128 heads × 128 dims make attention 16,384 wide inside, 2.29× of 7168; o_proj folds it back. Standard MHA needs the two equal; MLA drops that constraint.

Hence o_proj is the largest weight in an MLA layer (117.44M, 63% of it): it spans the 16384 → 7168 gap. Point back here at Step 2's five-row table.

Why is the vocabulary such an odd number, 129,280?

Certain: vocabulary size is set when training the tokenizer, as a trade-off. Bigger: one word per token, shorter and faster sequences, but both big matrices at entrance and exit grow. Smaller: the reverse. 129,280 is in between.

Checkable: 129,280 ÷ 128 = 1,010 exactly, friendly to the matrix units.

Unknown, so say so: DeepSeek has never publicly explained the choice. If asked: “They haven't said; I'm not sure.” Don't improvise a plausible reason.

What's the MTP row?

num_nextn_predict_layers = 1: besides the next token, the model also predicts the one after. Just mention it; Step 6 goes deeper. Its uses in training and inference differ completely; that's the interesting part.

Why aren't the first 3 layers MoE?

Good question; hold it. Never answer offhand “early layers are more general.” That's everywhere, but it isn't the paper's reason.

For now, one line: the earliest public reason was training stability (DeepSeekMoE saw layer-1 load balancing converge especially slowly), and the paper doesn't explain why V3 went from 1 layer to 3.

Step 3 takes it apart, with its own figure.

What not to say

The two easiest ways to drift here ① Don't explain fields one by one, least of all MLA's six (q_lora_rank, kv_lora_rank and the rest). They only make sense in Step 2. Explain now and you explain twice; the first won't land.
② Don't explain YaRN. If asked about the max_position_embeddings row, say “an extrapolation method stretches it from 4K to about 160K; that's Topic 3” and move on.
Pacing Two minutes. Hand out the model card as a later reference; don't tour the model. Past three minutes, you've drifted.
02

How a word becomes numbers

⏱ 8 min
What this segment must leave behind Before the first layer, a chunk of HBM is already gone. Nothing “computed” yet, just one table lookup, but 3.48 GiB is already spent: the lecture's first “so that's how the bill builds up” moment.

Script

Scroll to “Step 1 · From text to vectors”: vocabulary matrix on the left, residual-stream tensor on the right, a “lookup” arrow between.

First, what happens at the input. A sentence comes in and is cut into tokens.

A token isn't a character, nor a word. Common Chinese characters are mostly one token each; a long English word may split into two or three. Once cut, every token is just an integer ID, on V3 from 0 to 129,279: the vocabulary is 129,280.

Scroll to the axis figure under “Tokenization: why 129,280”: green band on top, red below, axis between: the tug-of-war over vocabulary size. Talk to the figure. Don't read the text.

The room usually asks first: “where does 129,280 come from?” The answer first: it's not special at all. Look at this axis. Llama 2 is 32K. Llama 3 is 128K, V3 is 129K, Qwen3 is 152K, gpt-oss about 200K, and Gemma 2 is 256K. V3 and Llama 3 are practically the same point.

Point at the green bandA bigger vocabulary buys this: fewer tokens per sentence. Shorter sequences, so attention's square term drops. That pays off in Step 2.

Point at the red bandIt costs three things: both tables grow, rare words get too few samples to learn, and the exit computes softmax over the whole vocabulary.

Get this one exactly right. The “both tables get bigger” cost barely applies to V3. Table size is vocabulary × width only, regardless of the 61 layers. So the bigger the model, the smaller its share: both tables together are only 0.28% of 671B. The other two costs are what really cap vocabulary size.

Pause. Next: this segment's first counterintuitive point.

By default, going from ID to vector isn't a multiplication. It's a lookup.

The model stores a big 129,280 × 7,168 table. Whatever your token's ID, just pull out that row: no matmul, no additions, one row fetched by index.

It's the plainest step in the model, the only one with almost no compute. Not cheap, though, because of the table: 129,280 times 7,168 is 926.68 million parameters, which is 1.73 GiB in bf16.

Point at the “Residual stream” tensor.

Now the second cost. After the lookup, each of the 128K tokens is a 7,168-dim vector. Stacked: a 131,072 × 7,168 tensor. Two bytes each in bf16: 1.75 GiB.

Add the two together here. Slow down.

A table, 1.73. A tensor, 1.75. Together, 3.48 GiB. And we haven't entered layer 1 yet.

This segment lands here. Stop two seconds; let it sink in.

Point at the main road running through the page (the residual stream figure).

One last thing; it shapes how you see the next 61 layers.

That 1.75 GiB tensor isn't scratch you throw away. It is the residual stream: one main road from layer 1 to layer 61. Every layer reads it once, computes, and writes back once.

So 7,168 isn't some layer's internal size but the main road's width, end to end. Hence “if you remember one number, make it this one.” Every later step's activation memory is tokens × 7,168 × bytes per number.

If someone asks how 7,168 was chosen, open the “hard constraint vs. DeepSeek's own call” block and talk from it. Otherwise leave it shut.

The block in one sentence: 7,168 divides by 128 and by 256. TPU matrix units are 128 or 256 a side; NVIDIA FP16 wants dimensions aligned to 8, 64 on A100. Neither side needs padding.

Then add at once: that only explains why not 7,000 or 7,200, not why not 6,144 or 8,192, which divide just as evenly. Wide versus deep: DeepSeek has never publicly said how they decided.

Likely questions

Isn't a lookup the same as multiplying a one-hot vector by a matrix?

Same math; in engineering, worlds apart. A one-hot vector is 129,280 long. Stack 128K tokens and you get a 131,072 × 129,280 matrix. That intermediate alone is 31.56 GiB, and 99.999% of it is zeros.

So the default fetches rows by index: same math, different algorithm, costs orders of magnitude apart. FlashAttention is the same idea.

(Don't say “always”: the page's Expand block notes 34 MaxText production configs turn on use_iota_embed, which really compiles to a one-hot matmul. The denominator is so large that it's only 0.46% of total compute.)

What about position? Is “which token is this” encoded here?

No. On V3 this step carries content only. RoPE injects position on the fly in Step 2's attention (config row qk_rope_head_dim = 64).

Early Transformers did add a positional encoding to the embedding; mainstream models have mostly moved it into attention. Why is Topic 3. Just mention it.

Does that 1.73 GiB table stay in HBM the whole time?

Yes. It's a weight, resident from load to unload.

The exit table is a separate copy, not the same memory. That's what tie_word_embeddings = false means. Two 926.68M tables, entrance and exit, 3.45 GiB together. This number comes back in Step 6.

If a bigger vocabulary has so many benefits, why not go to a million?

What holds up first. A vocabulary scaling-law paper (Scaling Laws with Vocabulary, NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2407.13623) concludes “most LLMs' vocabularies are too small.” Its fit puts Llama2-70B's optimum at 216K or more, vs. an actual 32K. Same compute, vocabulary 32K → 43K lifts ARC-Challenge 29.1 → 32.0.

⚠️ But state the limits: that paper fits dense models from 33M to 3B. There's no basis for extrapolating it to a 671B MoE. It shows the direction. It can't prove 129,280 is optimal for V3.

As for “why not a million”: rare words end up as dead words. It has a name, under-trained / glitch tokens (arXiv 2405.05417; the famous SolidGoldMagikarp is one; hit one and the model outputs garbage). Plus the exit softmax keeps getting more expensive.

You only get 1.75 GiB if you cram in the full 128K, right? Who has inputs that long?

Fair, and exactly the pattern we want them to find. Drag the console's sequence length to 4K; let them watch it fall. 56 MiB at 4K, 1.75 GiB at 128K, exactly 32×, because it's linear.

Linear is actually the good news. In Step 2 we'll see something that grows with the square, and that's the real trouble. Plant that hook here.

What not to say

The four easiest ways to drift here ① Skip tokenization algorithms (BPE merges, vocabulary training): off our thread (the math); ten minutes gone. If asked: “the tokenizer is trained separately; not covered.”
② Skip “why embeddings capture meaning.” King − man + woman is nice, but it's why the model works, not how to count its resources; we stay out of that.
③ Don't say “7,168 must divide by the head count.” (Flagged last segment.) V3's 128 heads × 128 dims make attention 16,384 wide inside, nowhere near 7,168. MLA removed that old constraint.
④ Don't turn “hardware-aligned” into “so they chose 7168.” Alignment is a hard constraint; width vs. depth, a trade-off: 6,144 and 8,192 divide by 128 and 256 just as evenly. Call a constraint a reason and any expert stops you with one question.
Pacing Eight minutes, but the real weight is in the last two. One minute on the vocabulary axis (“129,280 isn't special”), five on the lookup and the two costs, the last two on the residual stream as one main road. That's the skeleton of the whole topic. Go slowly.
By the end the room should think: “Only Step 1, and already 3.48 GiB?” If they feel that, Step 2 is easy to teach.

03

Why the KV cache runs away

⏱ 4 min
What this segment must leave behind Multiply two linear terms and you get a disaster. Don't give the answer here. Lay out the math and let the room conclude on its own that “this has to change.”

Script

Scroll to “The weak spot: a runaway KV cache.”

First, why it exists. Text is generated one token at a time, and each new token looks back at every token before it.

Store nothing, and each new token recomputes the tens of thousands before it. Again for the second, again for the third. The further you go, the worse the deal.

The natural fix: store each earlier token's intermediate results and reuse them. The two stored things are K and V; where they live is the KV cache. Nothing wrong there; every model does it.

Stop. Now: “where the trouble is.”

The trouble is how big it gets, and it grows in two directions at once.

One: the longer the sequence, the more you store. 100K tokens, 100K copies.
Two: every layer stores its own copy. 61 layers, 61 times.

Neither is scary alone. But they multiply. Grow linearly in two directions at once, and the product is a disaster.

Point at the log-scale staircase; walk it bar by bar.

“Disaster” is vague. Let's build the number step by step, assuming for now V3 uses the most traditional attention, unoptimized.

First bar: one token, one layer. 32,768 values to store: K and V × 128 heads × 128 dims per head. In bf16, 64 KiB. Negligible.

Second bar: × 61 layers. One token, whole model: 3.8 MiB. Still harmless.

Third bar: × 131,072 tokens. 488 GiB.

Stop for two seconds. This segment's first blowup.

Point at the vertical axis: log scale, 10× per gridline. It has to be, or the first bar would be invisible. Left to right spans nearly 8 million times, from two very ordinary steps: multiply by layers, multiply by tokens.

Point at the red dashed line, then at the number in the red box below.

Make it tangible. One TPU v7 device has 94.74 GiB of HBM. This pile needs more than five devices just to hold. For one user, one conversation. Serve a hundred people at once: times a hundred.

The sharper version, worth memorizing:

Stored this way, a single conversation of 25,446 tokens fills an entire device with cache alone. And not one byte of model weights has been loaded yet.

Scroll to the flare figure.

Last, how it changes with length. X is sequence length, Y is KV cache. Red is the traditional scheme; blue is MLA.

Both are straight, both linear, but their slopes differ by 56.9×. From one origin they keep spreading apart. The red gap between them is what a different storage scheme saves.

Say it out loud: not a fixed amount, but a gap that widens with length. The longer the context, the bigger the payoff from storing it differently. That's why, once long context arrived, everyone started changing attention.

Segment ends here. Don't go on to how MLA works.

Closing line: “At this point, I don't need to tell you what has to happen next.” Pause, and let them fill it in.

Likely questions

Why not skip the cache and recompute every time? Trade compute for memory.

Possible, but so costly nobody does it. Without a cache, token n recomputes the n before it: total compute goes from linear to quadratic.

And it's the wrong direction: generation is already bound by memory bandwidth, not compute. You'd spend the scarcer resource to save the cheaper one.

Partial recomputation is real; in training it's called recomputation. Different topic; skip it.

If it doesn't fit, can you move it to CPU memory or disk?

Yes: offloading, a mature practice. But it swaps “doesn't fit” for “moves too slowly”: every generated token hauls this pile across once, and bandwidth becomes the new bottleneck.

That's inference systems; this topic does the math, not solutions. Say “that's another route, and the cost is bandwidth,” and move on.

Store the KV in fp8 and you halve it, right?

Right, and widely used. Switch the console to fp8: 488 GiB becomes 244 GiB.

Make clear it's not the same thing as MLA: lowering precision stores each number smaller; MLA stores fewer numbers. The two stack, and real systems often use both.

Lower precision costs quality. How far you can go is an experimental question; this course draws no conclusion.

What about bigger batches? Serving lots of people at once?

One more multiplier, linear. Drag the batch slider to 8: the number goes up 8×.

The real consequence: the KV cache is one copy per user, the weights one copy shared by all. So the more people you serve, the bigger the KV cache's share of HBM, until it overtakes the weights themselves.

What not to say

The three easiest ways to drift here ① Never reveal how MLA works early. This segment's value is pushing people to “this has to change,” then stopping. Answer early and the next segment falls flat.
② Skip PagedAttention and memory fragmentation. That's inference-system engineering, not “why is the number so big,” and it won't stop once started.
③ Don't blur KV cache and weights. Keep repeating: the weights are one copy shared by everyone; the KV cache is one copy per user. The Step 7 tally needs it again.
Pacing Four minutes, but the turning point of the whole topic. The first three segments introduce. Here, for the first time, something goes wrong.
One minute per figure. The staircase shows how the number builds; the flare, how it spreads with length. After the closing line, stop. Two seconds of silence beat three more sentences.
04

Three knobs, four generations

⏱ 4 min
What this segment must leave behind The first three generations trade quality for memory. MLA trades compute for memory. Last segment: “something has to change”; this one: only a handful of places can. Narrower search, and the next segment's details land.

Script

Scroll to “Three knobs, four generations.”

We stopped at “I don't need to tell you what comes next.” Lay it out, with a reassurance: only a few places can change; you can count them.

Split the last formula's middle term: values per token per layer = 2 × KV heads × head dim. The 2 is K and V, fixed. The other two plus layer count are all the knobs.

Walk the three-row table row by row.

① Copies: KV heads; V3 has 128. The knob people keep turning, the strongest: to 8, divide by 16; to 1, by 128. The cost is as blunt: many Q heads share one K and V, and lost expressiveness never comes back.

② Width: the head dim. The industry settled on 128, for two reasons. It sets how much one head holds; narrow it and every head gets dumber. And kernels are tuned for few widths. You save memory but run slowly.

③ Layers: neighboring layers share one copy, or only some see the full length. This knob differs: it changes whether you store, not how much, so it's not in the four-generation table. If asked, one sentence.

Stop. Three knobs done; now four generations. Scroll to the four-generation figure. Point at each card, left to right.

The four generations: knob one turned from 128 down to 1.

MHA, 2017. Original Transformer: one KV per head. Best quality, biggest cache.

MQA, 2019. A very short Shazeer paper, “One write-head is all you need”: all heads share one copy, dividing straight by the head count.

Say this; it explains the next decade. Shazeer solved not “it doesn't fit” but “it can't move fast enough”: each generated token rereads K and V from HBM. Bandwidth was the bottleneck. “Doesn't fit” came later, with long context.

GQA, 2023. Ainslie et al. Neither one copy nor one per head: shared within groups. Default for a practical reason: the paper's recipe converts an MHA model to GQA with 5% of the original pre-training compute. No training from scratch.

Short pause. The punchline. Point at the blue card, far right.

Now look at MLA. It turns none of these knobs.

Still 128 heads. Still 128 dims each. All 61 layers still there. It changes what's stored: not expanded K and V, but a 512-dim compressed vector plus 64 position dims on their own path. 576 values in all.

Back to the numbers on the four-generation cards.

576 sits between GQA's 2,048 and MQA's 256. Yet its quality beats both. (Inferred from two tables: MHA beats GQA and MQA; MLA beats MHA.)

Stop; let them spot what's wrong. On one axis, that's impossible. Save more than GQA and beat its quality? No free lunch. It works because MLA isn't on that axis at all.

Two-second pause. Land it.

So the takeaway isn't the four names. It's one sentence:

The first three trade quality for memory; MLA, compute for memory.

The first three discard information for good. MLA compresses it, expanding into 128 heads on the spot. Nothing lost; the price is extra compute per use.

Trainers know this: don't store the intermediate, recompute it: recomputation. In a sense, MLA is recomputation for the KV cache.

Point at the yellow warning box.

One boundary you must add. Omit it and you mislead; get it wrong, worse. “MLA saves memory” is an inference statement: training has no KV cache at all. Caching exists only in decode, keeping earlier K and V for the next token. Training runs the whole sequence in one forward pass: no next step, nothing to cache.

Training's bill is activation memory, and at this scale recomputation is maxed out: a layer keeps only its input residual stream; 128-head K and V are recomputed, used, discarded. So MHA and MLA store the same residual stream: a tie, not an MLA win.

Don't flip to “MLA is useless for training”, the opposite mistake. Training gains twice, both in Topic 4: relax recomputation a notch and store just the 512-dim latent; context parallelism passes the latent, not K and V, cutting communication by the same factor.

Hand off: “Compress to 512, expand to 128 heads. Sounds like magic. Next: how, and why 64 position dims need their own path.”

Likely questions

MQA existed in 2019. Why did large-scale use wait until 2023?

Stress this: to keep that rewrite possible, RoPE had to be isolated. The design made room for an optimization that hadn't happened yet: this section's most intriguing point.

The weights are a few big matrices. So are the 128 heads real, or just bookkeeping?

Half right. In projections, bookkeeping. In scoring, heads are physical, unmergeable.

The right half: q_b_proj, kv_b_proj, o_proj are each one block, not 128. view: zero data movement, zero compute. “Head i's matrix” is its i-th column group.

The wrong half, as a counterfactual: no heads: one dot product of full 24,576-dim Q and K, one score per token pair. With 128 heads, each pair gets 128 scores, each with its own softmax, each weighting its own slice of V.

Totally different, and the second can't be one big matmul: softmax is nonlinear. Linear ops merge any way; apply a nonlinearity per group and they can't be re-stitched.

So a head has a lifecycle: at view it gets a name, it truly lives in scoring and softmax, at o_proj it dies. That matrix is the only place the 128 heads ever exchange info.

Hardware angle (infra folks love it): projections are big regular matmuls, accelerators' favorite; scoring, many small ones, low arithmetic intensity: one reason attention was long a bottleneck.

Why divide by root 192, and not root 128?

Because the dot product runs over 192 dims. The scale follows the dot product's dims, not the content part.

In code it's q_head_dim ** -0.5, with q_head_dim = 128 + 64 = 192.

One 7,168 by 24,576 matrix can express more. Pinch a 1,536 waist in the middle, and it gets at most 1,536 independent directions. In math terms, its rank is capped.

Analogy: a junction box with 7,168 wires in and 24,576 wires out, but only 1,536 between. Anything gets through, just not as many patterns.

Stricter: there's an RMSNorm in the middle. So it isn't even a product of two matrices; the middle step is nonlinear.

Real savings: direct takes 176M parameters, split 48.76M, 3.61× fewer. DeepSeek bets the transformation never needed that much rank.

Likely questions

Is “bus” a metaphor, or literally true?

Close to literal, which is why I like it.

That 7,168-dim vector sits in HBM the whole forward pass. Every layer reads and adds to that same block of memory.

Unlike a bus, layers access it strictly in order: no contention. Most accurately: “a bus accessed sequentially.”

If every layer is an optional increment, can you just prune some layers?

Structurally, yes; that's this segment's property. The shape doesn't change, so remove a layer and the rest still run.

But quality drops, by a hard-to-predict amount. What it learned isn't backed up elsewhere.

“Can be pruned” isn't “pruning is harmless.” Layer pruning is real, but that's model compression.

Pre-norm vs. post-norm: what differs?

Whether the norm touches the trunk.

Pre-norm: normalize, run the sublayer, add its output straight back. The trunk is pure addition, never rescaled.

Post-norm: add, then normalize the whole trunk, so every layer touches that gradient highway once.

With enough layers, post-norm trains unstably. Same as the first reason earlier: no toll booths on the highway.

For something harder: Xiong et al. 2020 (On Layer Normalization in the Transformer Architecture) analyzed gradients at initialization. Post-norm has large gradients near the output layer, so warm-up is required; pre-norm's are well-behaved, so warm-up can go entirely.

Note the claim's shape: not “post-norm can't be trained,” but “post-norm adds one more thing to get right, harder to tune with depth.” At tens to hundreds of layers that means “unstable training,” but repeat it precisely.

(Skip norm variants; the course needs one intuition: keep the trunk clean.)

How is 106.75 GiB computed? Why only one copy per layer?

131,072 tokens × 7,168 dims × 2 bytes (bf16): one copy is 1.75 GiB. Times 61 layers, 106.75 GiB.

“One copy per layer” is a floor. Real implementations also keep intermediates inside each layer, so the true number is only higher.

The floor persuades better: even the leanest accounting doesn't fit.

If asked “how much higher”: about 4.15 TiB, roughly forty times. The bulk: MoE copies each token's activations nine times, and MLA decompresses K and V back to over 30,000 dims. That compression saves inference KV cache, not the training forward pass. That's Topic 4: give the magnitude and stop.

Do V4's four residual streams make the model four times wider?

No. Be clear, or people hear “four times the parameters.”

The residual stream widens, not each layer's compute width. Sublayers still read and write 7,168; only the channels between layers multiply. The V4 paper's own words: “the expanded residual width does not influence the design of the inner layers.”

Numbers you can give: both sizes use nhc = 4 and 20 Sinkhorn-Knopp iterations. V4-Pro has 61 layers and hidden 7,168, identical to V3, so this page's math applies directly. V4-Flash has 43 layers and 4,096.

4× wider, so 4× activation memory?

The easiest one to get wrong; we've done so ourselves.

“1.75 GiB × 4 = 7.00 GiB per layer” is an upper bound, not the cost paid. It assumes all four streams are kept whole at every layer.

The V4 paper is explicit: fused kernels, selective recompute, and a reworked DualPipe overlap. In their words, they “recompute most hidden states between layers and all normalized layer inputs.” A large share is never stored.

So the paper gives no absolute memory increase; it depends on recompute settings. Its one absolute number is time: mHC's wall-clock overhead is 6.7% of the overlapped 1F1B pipeline stage.

A quick aside: MTP

One more config entry. Besides the next token, the model also predicts the one after it. Note: one extra token. The depth is 1.

Likely questions

How can logits be over 30 GiB? Isn't the output matrix just 7168 × 129280?

The lecture's most common sticking point; someone will ask. Root cause: “position” is used in two places, and listeners merge them.

Agree first: that 7168 × 129280 matrix exists, and there's only one copy. It's the weights: 1.73 GiB, already counted in the 671B. They're right, but that's one token's story.

Then pull the dimensions apart: you fed in 128K tokens. Each token multiplies its own 7168-dim vector by that matrix and gets its own 129280 scores. So the result isn't one row but a table of 128K rows by about 129K columns. 252.5 KiB a row × 128K rows = 31.56 GiB.

On the whiteboard, draw two edges: horizontal “vocab 129,280”, vertical “tokens 131,072”. Say: “About the same size. A coincidence.” Always say this. Otherwise half the room thinks they're the same thing.

Close with a twist: in every earlier layer, a position is just 7168 numbers. At the exit it becomes 129,280: 18× fatter. The model is widest not in the middle but on the way out.

So is that why pretraining usually uses 4K, to keep the logits small?

Right conclusion, wrong reason. Correct it now or it sticks.

Logits depend only on the total token count, not on sequence length. Total tokens = sequence length × batch. 128K × batch 1 and 4K × batch 32 are both 131,072 tokens, so the logits are identical: 31.56 GiB either way. Shorter sequence, bigger batch: nothing saved.

What short sequences save is attention. It costs batch × the square of sequence length, so at fixed total tokens it's proportional to sequence length: the 128K split pays 32× the 4K split.

For an expert, give a checkable anchor: in V3's config, original_max_position_embeddings is 4096, and YaRN's factor is 40. 4096 × 40 = 163840. The base model was trained at 4K. 128K was extended afterward.

Stop there. Training upcasting logits to fp32, hence standard chunked cross-entropy, is Topic 4. Just name it.

Does that 31.56 GiB really get allocated, or is it just theoretical?

Not in a normal implementation, and that's the point, not a disclaimer.

It's the literal coding of the math definition. Nobody does that, because it's this big.

Its value isn't “a block this size sits in memory”; it explains why every framework chunks and fuses here. Without it, you don't know what that code is avoiding.

(Don't put it in a capacity plan. The real peak depends on your chunk size, not on this number.)

Why doesn't V3 share the input and output weights? Wouldn't sharing save memory?

It really does save: 926M parameters, 1.73 GiB. Sizable on small models, so nearly all of them tie.

At 671B, that copy is 0.14% of the total. Hardly worth it.

The case for not tying: reading and writing aren't really the same job. “What does this token mean?” and “should the next position be this token?” want different representations. Tying forces two jobs onto one parameter set.

(Don't claim this is why DeepSeek didn't tie. The report doesn't say; it's the common explanation.)

Does chunking the logits give a different result than computing them all at once?

Math: identical. Floating point: tiny differences.

Why identical: cross-entropy is independent per position. Slice by position, add up the losses, and it's the same expression as one pass.

The differences: summation order. Float addition isn't associative, so chunking may flip the last bits; harmless to training.

What needs care is softmax's own numerical stability. That's why you upcast to fp32 and subtract the max first.

It's only 0.46% of compute. Doesn't that make this step unimportant?

The opposite: that contrast is what this segment teaches.

Compute and memory are separate ledgers; here's the course's most extreme case. Near zero on compute, number one on memory.

Allocate by compute and you miss it, then OOM here.

(Point back to Step 4's “parameters and compute are separate ledgers.” Same idea, different ledgers.)

“tie = false, nothing shared,” but I heard V3's output head is shared?

Both true; different things are shared. The segment's easiest tangle.

Not shared: embedding table ↔ output head. That's tie_word_embeddings = false, which is why we count 926M twice.

Shared: the main model's output head ↔ the MTP module's output head. The paper puts the shallowest (embedding) and deepest (output head) layers on one pipeline rank, so MTP and the main model physically share those parameters and gradients.

So: MTP doesn't add another 926M to the total. That answers challenges to the Step 7 table.

63.13 GiB ÷ 94.74 gives me 67%. Didn't the page say 72%?

67% is right. The 72% was an old error on the page, kept there as a warning.

GiB is 1024³, GB is 1000³: a 7.4% gap. The old text assumed 94.74 was decimal GB, converted 63.13 GiB to 67.78 GB, and got 72%.

But 94.74 was GiB all along: the compiler string 95.38G − 94.74G = 656.93M only works in base 1,024. Both numbers share a base, so divide directly: 63.13 ÷ 94.74 = 67%.

Every percentage on the page is computed from bytes, not from the displayed numbers. Worth saying live: the trap isn't ignorance of units but knowing the trap and fixing the wrong side. Check the denominator's base.

Why is the vocabulary as big as 129,280? Wouldn't a smaller one save memory?

Vocabulary size is a two-sided trade-off.

Bigger: a word fits in one token, sequences get shorter and faster, but the embedding table and logits both grow.

Smaller: you save on those two, but the same text needs more tokens, and compute grows with them; the attention term quadratically.

So shrinking it usually loses. You save a linear term and pay a quadratic one.

(Don't compare vendors' vocabularies. “A trade-off, and the sides aren't symmetric” is enough.)

Likely questions

In the peak-activations table, why is it “residual stream ×2”?

Heads-up: “×2” appears three times in that row, for different reasons. Don't lump them. Q/K one each, V/output one each: just two same-size tensors. Only the residual stream's ×2 needs explaining.

A layer first keeps a copy of the incoming x as is, then layer-norms x into a separate buffer. The sublayer computes on that, and the result is added back onto the kept copy.

The add-back is key: because of it, the original x can't be dropped. From the norm until the addition, two 1.75 GiB copies are live at once. That's the ×2.

The MoE layer repeats the same structure, so it's also ×2. The output step has no add-back, so it's one copy, as the table shows. Sharp eyes will spot it; use it as your opening.

Config diff in detail (18 tile keys; why the original's 1024 isn't its real tile)
  • Both configs have all 18 tile keys; the other 6, *_batch_seq, are 512 on both sides. Only the 12 above and use_tokamax_gmm actually changed.
  • On the tokamax path, which the original recipe takes, the tile isn't read from these 12 keys; the launch script injects it, and the compiled program says (512, 2048, 1536). So “1024 → 2048” doesn't mean the kernel's real tiling changed.

0.3 When in doubt, estimate first

⭐ Good news isn't necessarily fake, or necessarily real. Before opening the profile, spend a minute estimating: is this number possible on this machine, for this kind of model? Estimate it, and you'll know what to look for once it's open.

1.1 Ruler one: MFU

MFU = achieved TFLOP/s per chip ÷ peak. v7 BF16 peak: 2,307 TFLOP/s per chip. FP8 FLOPs are still counted from model shapes (a multiply-add = 2 ops, regardless of bytes) over the same 2,307, so both precisions share one ruler.

The modified row leaps out of the MoE cluster, a notch below dense. How to tell: TFLOP/s per chip = MaxText's per-device FLOPs per step (3,981.54 TFLOP) × 2 (devices per chip) ÷ step time. Both FP8 runs log identical FLOPs, so the MFU gain is all from the shorter step.

MFU denominators: 2,307, 2,057.5, 4,614 (same run, new denominator, new number)
  • 2,307: BF16 peak per chip, used by MaxText and this course; per device, half that: 1,153.5.
  • 2,057.5: XProf's Roofline Model page gives 1,028.75 TFLOP/s per TensorCore; × 2 that is 2,057.5 per chip, 10.8% below 2,307. Unify denominators before comparing XProf utilization with MaxText MFU.
  • 4,614: FP8 peak per chip. Divide by it and FP8 MFU is artificially halved (1,014.8 ÷ 4,614 = 22.0%). Always state FP8 MFU's denominator.
  • Sources: TPU7x (Ironwood) specs (per chip BF16 2,307 and FP8 4,614 TFLOP/s, 2 TensorCores) from public Google Cloud docs, cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/tpu7x; TUNING-v7 §⁠1.1 (denominator), §⁠1.2 (public measured table), §⁠2.7 (XProf constants); 1,014.8 = 2 × 3,981.54 ÷ 7.847 and 677.0 = 2 × 3,981.54 ÷ 11.761 (our recomputation).
Compute it yourself (two numbers from MaxText's TensorBoard events; eight lines of Python) 
import sys, statistics
from tensorboard.backend.event_processing.event_accumulator import EventAccumulator
ea = EventAccumulator(sys.argv[1]); ea.Reload()
v = lambda k: [e.value for e in ea.Scalars(k)]
t = statistics.median(v("perf/step_time_seconds")[2:])      # skip 2 steps; median avoids warm-up and the profile write
f = v("perf/per_device_tflops")[-1]                         # FLOPs per device per step (TFLOP)
chip = 2 * f / t                                            # 2 devices per v7 chip
print(f"step time {t:.3f} s  per chip {chip:,.1f} TFLOP/s  MFU {chip / 2307:.1%}")
  • Run it on the original and modified event files: 676.2 (29.3%) and 1,013.7 (43.9%). The 0.1% gap from the table is from picking different steps (the table uses the tuning log's step times).
  • Sanity check: an MFU above 100% means a wrong timing basis or overcounted FLOPs.

1.2 Ruler two: the roofline

The roofline bounds an op's speed. X-axis: arithmetic intensity (operations per byte moved from HBM). Bandwidth caps the left slope, peak compute the flat roof; they meet at the ridge point.

FP8 shrinks a number from 2 bytes to 1, a 2× gain at either end of the chart. Under the flat roof, the roof rises 2×; on the slope, half the bytes moves the point 2× right, also exactly 2× faster. So a matmul gains at most 2×, and only matmuls gain: routing, per-expert token permutes, elementwise ops and communication aren't on this chart.

Where the right curve comes from (1 ÷ (1 − p/2), solved for p)
  • Step time = matmuls p + the rest (1 − p). At best FP8 halves matmuls, rest unchanged: new step = p/2 + (1 − p), a 1 ÷ (1 − p/2) speedup.
  • Conversely, a measured speedup s means matmuls take at least p ≥ 2 × (1 − 1/s): DeepSeek-V3 s = 1.214 → p ≥ 35%; dense 405B s = 1.528 → p ≥ 69%; Hy3 modified s = 1,014.8 ÷ 662.3 = 1.532 → p ≥ 69%.
  • This assumes FP8 touches only matmuls. The original recipe also quantizes expert weights to FP8 before the all-gather, halving those bytes too. But in this profile, exposed communication is only about 0.2 s per step; counting it doesn't change the conclusion.

Public results report the per-chip TFLOP/s gain from BF16 to FP8 on the same model, so Hy3 also starts from its own BF16 (662.3). Section 0's 1.50× starts from the FP8 original recipe instead.

  • Of the 80 layers, the first is dense and the other 79 are MoE, weights stacked with leading dim 79. “Layer N” in this course always counts MoE layers (0–78), leaving out the dense one. Why only while gets sub-levels in XLA Ops, and how to count its iterations (three small whiles inside one layer)

Most HLO instructions run on the hardware as one block: a fusion compiles to one kernel, and the sub-computations of sort or reduce (just rules for comparing or adding two values) go into that same kernel. Only while runs its body iteration by iteration at runtime; each body instruction logs an event, so they stack beneath it as sub-levels. JAX’s lax.scan, fori_loop and while_loop all compile to while, as do library functions that use scan inside. A Python for unrolls at compile time: no while.

4.1 HLO: the program after compilation

⭐ HLO is your JAX program after XLA has partitioned, fused and scheduled it: one instruction per line, a shape on every value. Every slice name in the trace is an HLO instruction name.

HLO (the IR of XLA's High Level Optimizer) is the compiler's internal program form. You write a train_step; the compiler splits it across 64 devices, fuses elementwise chains into fusions, inserts collectives and fixes the order, giving the final HLO, one line like this per instruction:

name = type[shape] opcode(operands, …), attributes…
gmm.27 = bf16[229376,1536] custom-call(…, get-tuple-element.14549, …), custom_call_target="tpu_custom_call"

The second line is the gmm.27 from Sections 2 and 3: a bf16 output of 229,376 × 1,536, and custom-call means a call into an external kernel (the Pallas expert matmul). The trace tells you when each instruction runs and for how long; the HLO, what goes in and out and which instructions exist at all, including ones never generated, which no profile shows.

4.7 After the fix

⭐ With the gather and scatter moved out of the tokamax branch so both branches share them, a step takes 12.501 s, 6% slower than the original’s 11.761 s. The “50% faster” was all skipped work.

Why errors like this stay silent The 192-expert dimension is split 3 per device, and 3 experts is a fully legal grouped-matmul input: the kernel just sees 3 groups.

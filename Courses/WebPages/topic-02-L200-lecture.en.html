Scroll one screen to the Section 0 figure. It has just two boxes. Nothing else to find. Make those two boxes clear, then move on.

Here's the first figure. It's the lecture's only premise: these two boxes.

On the left, the GPU. It started as a graphics processor. From day one it had to serve tons of small, unrelated jobs. It doesn't know the shapes, the access patterns, or the control flow. So most of its die goes to handling any case: the cache hierarchy, the warp schedulers, the huge thread counts.

On the right, the TPU. It started as an ASIC built for matrix multiply, serving only neural networks from day one. Shapes are regular, access is predictable, control flow is static. So it needs none of that “handle any case” circuitry.

⛔ The easiest slip, and I've made it myself: don't say “all the saved area went into compute.”
The savings became neither compute nor on-chip storage. Section 3b rules out both with two tables: the GPU has 16% more total MACs and 73% more on-chip SRAM.

So what did it save? Complexity itself. No cache, no scheduler, layout fixed at compile time. The same compute in four big blocks, not five hundred ninety-two small ones. That's a hundred forty-eight times fewer sets of control circuitry.

⚠️ Someone will likely ask “So is it more power-efficient?” There's a solid official answer. Give it with its caveats.

Yes, you can answer. Two things suffice. First, a quote from the first TPU paper. It lists everything left out: no caches, no branch prediction, no out-of-order execution, no multithreading, no context switching. The next sentence: “Minimalism is a virtue of domain-specific processors.”

⭐ Second, a number, which beats any adjective: “On that die, control logic takes just 2%.”
Data buffers 37%, compute 30%, I/O 10%. Control: two percent. The paper adds that this part is much larger on CPUs and GPUs. ⛔ Then brake. All three caveats, every time:
One: the paper compares the 2015 TPU v1 with the K80. Back then the savings did become compute: a small die with 25× the MACs. For v7 vs. B200, Section 3b counts the GPU at 16% more. Same mechanism; ten years later, the opposite outcome.
Two: Ironwood's official 2× perf per watt is vs. the previous TPU, not GPUs.
Three: yield and reliability. No source, so don't say it. The official word is “deterministic execution”: tail latency, not reliability. Don't mix them. One beat. The next two lines are this segment's memory line.

One doesn't know what you'll run, so it hedges at every step. The other knows from day one, so it hedges nothing.

No more scrolling. Stay on the two boxes.

So what is this lecture about? The two sides differ everywhere. But every difference follows from a single decision.

Which decision? When a block of data moves from HBM onto the chip, who schedules it. On the GPU, a cache guesses for you at runtime. The TPU has no cache; it's fixed at compile time.

Shapes padded to 8 by 128, how big a bite one instruction takes, who writes the kernels, even what wiring a whole machine costs: it all grows from that one decision.

Slow down. This is the lecture's claim; every later section is evidence for it. Rush it and it sounds like a slogan.

One more thing, and I mean it: this lecture doesn't rank the chips. Both origins were the right call at the time, and each still carries its cost. You won't hear “which is better” today. Only “who decides this, on this side.”

That's the foundation. Straight into Section 1. It's one division, so it's quick. After that, Section 2 starts taking the hardware apart.

Likely questions

“Only one cause”: isn't that oversimplifying?

There are a dozen-odd symptoms. “One” means one cause. Caches, shape alignment, who writes kernels, interconnect wiring: all really differ, but they're not a dozen parallel facts. They're a dozen consequences of one decision.

At the opening it's a claim, not a proven conclusion. Say so: “You don't have to believe it yet. Sections 3 and 5 each test it.” That convinces more than digging in, and gives them a reason to keep listening.

Does the TPU really have no cache at all? Then what is VMEM?

Precisely: the TPU has no hardware-managed cache tier. It has on-chip storage, plenty of it, but as a software-managed scratchpad: when data arrives, where it goes and when it leaves are all fixed by the compiler. Nothing decides anything at runtime.

That distinction is the main thread, so the question is a gift: “Right. What's missing isn't capacity, it's who decides.”
⚠️ Don't go into VMEM size or partitioning at the opening. Section 3 has a figure for it.

Why make FlashAttention the star? Why not a GEMM, or a whole model?

It meets two conditions that rarely hold together.

First, it runs on one chip. No inter-chip communication, so the story runs on one line (which is why Section 4, on inter-chip links, never mentions it).

Second, it hits four things at once: memory access, shapes, on-chip capacity, kernel authorship. A pure GEMM is too clean to touch the last three. A whole model is too messy: every step changes the subject, and nothing lands.

Section 4 has nothing to do with FlashAttention. Why is it in the core track?

It really is off the FlashAttention thread. The test is hard: FlashAttention appears 0 times in Section 4's main text (grepped). It finishes on one chip and never touches inter-chip links.

But it underpins Section 6's measurements: those two numbers come from 64 vs. 64 chips, and the way each side wires 64 chips together is structurally different. Without this section, Section 6's ratio can't be read. So it stays in the core track; don't skip it whole. Short on time, cut it to two sentences per the emergency list at the top.

I just said there's only one root cause. But before comparing anything, we need this machine's exchange rate between computing and moving data. That's all this section does: get that number, no conclusions.

Both spec sheets give two numbers: operations per second and bytes moved per second. Divide one by the other. The ratio is very concrete: in the time it takes to move one byte, how many operations can this hardware do?

This is easy to say backwards. Slow down. Let's measure two right now.

First, a vector add. y = a + b, in bf16. Read a, two bytes. Read b, two bytes. Write y, two bytes. Six bytes moved. How many operations? One add. So the intensity is 1 over 6, or 0.17.

Against 312, that's over 1,800 times too low.

Second, a matmul. Square, N by N, bf16. The operation count is 2 N cubed. Moving three matrices in and out is 6 N squared bytes. Cancel, and the intensity is N over 3.

Heads usually come up here. It overturns something almost everyone assumes. Same figure, column ③. These forty seconds start the lecture's through-line. Don't rush.

Now we can name the real protagonist of this lecture.

Those two bits of arithmetic said one thing: whether an op is fast depends on how many operations it does per byte moved. So there's exactly one way to make a slow op fast: shrink the denominator. Move less.

And the best way to move less is to merge adjacent ops into one kernel, so intermediates never touch HBM. That's fusion.

Let it land: it doesn't save a single FLOP. The numerator stays put; only the denominator changes. Many people think fusion means computing less. It doesn't.

So on this line, fusion pushes the op to the right, from the bandwidth side to the compute side.

Still column ③. Point at the yellow box in the lower half (312 → 64). Added because the last sentence gets misheard.

I said intermediates never touch HBM. That's easy to hear as “the intermediates disappear.” They don't.

They're parked in on-chip scratch: the shared memory on the GPU, up to 227 KB; VMEM on the TPU. HBM traffic really drops, but the data still has to land on chip.

Let it land: the data movement didn't disappear. It moved to another level. That's what the three bars were for.

And the on-chip level has its own line. We read it earlier: 64, not 312. So after fusing, redo the math: compare the new intensity with 64.

You must add three sentences here, or the last one contradicts itself: 312 to 64 lowers the bar. It doesn't raise it.

So why do you still hit a wall? Because the denominator moves too, not just the bar. Fusion turns HBM traffic into on-chip traffic. On-chip bytes can grow tens of times, while the bar drops only 5×. The wall comes from a bigger denominator, not a higher bar.

Someone usually asks: “So why not keep it all in registers?” Usually you can't. An SM's register file totals 256 KB, the same as shared memory. And every extra register you take means fewer resident warps. The numbers are in the SM breakdown figure in §3.2.

1.2 Square is a special case: the smallest dimension sets intensity (Figure 1-8)

Scroll to Figure 1-8. ⏱ 3 min here, already counted in §1's 10 minutes.

The matmuls in those examples were square: all sides N, giving N/3. But a real matmul is M×K · K×N. So which dimension sets the intensity?

Same division. Operations: 2 M N K. Bytes moved: 2 times MK plus KN plus MN. It simplifies very cleanly: one over intensity equals one over M, plus one over N, plus one over K.

Say “reciprocals add” on its own, once. It's the key to this part: reciprocals add, so the smallest one dominates.

So “longer K, higher intensity” is right, and monotonic. But there's a ceiling. That's what the five rows in column ② show: M and N fixed at 512, K from 64 up to a million, and intensity climbs from 51 to 256, then stops. The cap is MN over M plus N: exactly 256.

K can't rescue a small M or N. The harshest case: decode at batch size 1. M is 1, so intensity caps at 1, however big K and N get. That one line of arithmetic is why LLM decode is always stuck on bandwidth.

Now plug in attention. In Q times K transpose, both outer dimensions are the sequence length S, and the contraction dimension is head_dim. Most models use 128, so intensity is about 128. Below v7's 312 line, and below v6e's 560.

⭐ Say this out loud. It overturns a common assumption: “Attention is a matmul, so of course it's compute-bound” is a myth. Unfused, it sits on the bandwidth side, held there by head_dim.

So what does Flash change? Direction now; the next section's first figure does the full math: it doesn't save a single FLOP. It changes the denominator. The S-by-S matrix no longer touches HBM. Only Q, K, V and O cross it, so intensity becomes S over 2. With Wan's S of 75,600, that's 37,800.

Script · 5.1 Who owns latency

Scroll to the start of Section 5; stop at Figure G-7.

Every section so far stopped at the same decision, just on a different part. This section looks at the decision itself. Who schedules the work? That decision flows from the hardware all the way to your hand on the keyboard.

Start at the bottom, where it's clearest. A load takes hundreds of cycles. What happens meanwhile? The two answers differ not in speed, but in who's responsible.

The GPU's answer: one warp stalls, run the next. In this Gantt chart, dashed boxes are “waiting for data,” while other warps keep issuing.

“This only hides the wait if enough warps are resident. That's all ‘occupancy’ means.”

And registers set how many fit. At 32 registers per thread, all 64 warps fit. At 128, only 16, and it starts to pinch. That's why tuning often starts by cutting registers.

Switch to Figure T-7. Another pair. Point at them back to back.

The TPU's answer is completely different: the schedule is fixed at compile time and can't change at runtime.

A VLIW bundle is everything this cycle does, packed together: the scalar slot fires a DMA, the vector slot normalizes, the matrix slot pushes a multiply, the misc slot holds a wait. All issued in the same cycle.

How can it be that precise? Latency is a constant. No cache, so latency is constant. Constant, so every cycle can be filled at compile time.

Say these two back to back, two sides of one thing: “With a cache, latency isn't constant. Not constant, so the compiler can't schedule, so you need a big bench of stand-ins.
No cache, latency is constant. Constant, so you fill every slot, and need no stand-ins.”

⚠️ But look at the gray cells. Gray is truly empty, and nothing steps in automatically. The compiler either finds other work for it, or eats the loss.

Kind one: fusion that keeps the math. The compiler does it. Same expressions as before, just no trip to cold storage. You write zero lines for it. Both compilers do it silently.

Plain softmax can't fuse: the denominator sums the whole row. No output until you've seen the whole row, so you can't keep just a small block on the stovetop. Online softmax changes exactly that: update the running max and denominator as you go, rescale once at the end, identical results.

Slow down: “They didn't fuse first and tweak the algorithm on the side. They swapped the algorithm so fusion could work. The compiler decides how things are scheduled, not what's computed.”

⭐ So “who decides fusion?” has two halves:
Whether and how much: the hardware (§3.6's stovetop and blade width jointly cap it);
who fuses: depends on whether the math changes (no: the compiler; yes: only you).

That explains an apparent paradox: fusion is the one topic running through the whole course, yet it's neither purely hardware nor purely software. It sits right on the boundary, which is why studying hardware always ends up there.

Script · 5.4 Catching the objection

Scroll to Figure P-33. Two minutes. Defense, not offense.

Right about here, the strongest objection always comes up, and half of it is right: “Doesn't CUDA Graph make the GPU compile-time too?”

⭐ First, grant the right half in full. All the persuasion is here: “You record the whole step's kernel graph up front and run it straight through. The gains are real. On many workloads, on vs. off is an order of magnitude.”

But to judge what it overturns, be precise about why it's fast. The common explanation is wrong.

It doesn't save “branches.” It saves the CPU launching kernels one by one. One launch is about 5–10 microseconds on the host; an MoE step has tens of thousands of kernels. Launching alone takes tens of milliseconds. A graph turns all that into one replay.

Point at the figure's middle column. That's the answer.

So it comes down to which layer. Split the path in three: sequencing (which kernel next), placement (which SM gets a block), issue (which instruction next cycle).

CUDA Graph freezes only the first layer. The other two are untouched. Each graph node is still a normal kernel launch. Schedulers, scoreboards, warp slots, resident contexts: all still working.
The TPU freezes all three, the third down to the exact cycle and issue slot.

Three reasons. Pause on the third: “Hardware cost is set by the worst case it must support, not the case you run. Even if 100% of workloads used graphs, none of §3.2's five components could go. At tape-out, the chip doesn't know whether you'll turn graphs on.”

So the point worth making runs the other way: for this performance, the GPU has to become more like a TPU. And it can only move the top layer earlier. The two below can't move. If they could, those transistors would be long gone.

📌 If pressed—“what does CUDA Graph actually compile, who replays it, is the scoreboard still there?”—don't improvise. A collapsed block on the page answers exactly those four. Open it and read it. One line to memorize: CUDA Graph works on the outside of kernels. It doesn't know what's inside, so architecturally it can't reach the lower two layers.

Script · 5.4b The second objection: what about TMA?

Scroll to 5.4b. Two minutes. ⭐ This one deserves an answer more than CUDA Graph.

There's a second objection, far stronger, because it points the right way: “On data movement, isn't the GPU moving toward the TPU too?”

⭐ Concede it, gladly. The persuasion rests on conceding first. “Yes, and the trend is unmistakable. Read §3.3b's four-generation figure top to bottom.”

Always: plain loads. Data passes through registers, threads compute addresses, strides and bounds, and math waits for the copy.
From Ampere: no register hop, but threads still compute addresses.
From Hopper: TMA. One thread fills a descriptor, fires it, moves on. Hardware takes over strides, offsets and bounds. 32 lanes each computing an address: deliberately abandoned on this path.
From Blackwell: the biggest step. One thread issues matrix instructions for the whole CTA. Operands and accumulators move into a dedicated matrix scratchpad; results never return to the register file.

Hold a beat, then name the endpoint: “Four generations in, the path ends like this: whole blocks, by descriptor, no threads tied up, landing in a software-managed dedicated scratchpad.”
“That's exactly the TPU on day one.”

⭐ But the trend only went halfway. The other half is this course's main thread.

How data moves has converged: granularity, who generates addresses, where operands land. Those three really are heading to one endpoint.
Who decides hasn't converged; that layer hasn't moved a step: a TMA descriptor is built at runtime, not baked into the binary. L1 and L2 remain; hits are still known only at runtime. The warp scheduler still picks live, every cycle.

⭐⭐ Where the section lands. Slow down: “It's not ‘the GPU is becoming a TPU.’ It's ‘the GPU made feeding TPU-like, while the layer deciding who gives the orders hasn't moved.’ The split was never the endpoint. It's who issues the DMA.”

⚠️ Close with why the trend was inevitable, so nobody calls it copying: the faster the matrix unit, the less feeding can tie up the units doing math. One matrix instruction eats tens of KB in a few cycles. If 32 threads still take turns computing addresses, they become gatekeepers of their own compute.
Same physics problem, same answer. One vendor took four generations. The other shipped at the endpoint.

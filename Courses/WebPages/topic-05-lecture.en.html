🔍 Going deeper: why these five things, the two mountains, how 318 years becomes two months (if pressed or time allows; not in the floor)

Why five, not one weight copy? Weights and gradients go half precision for speed: matmuls are the heaviest work; it computes faster and halves the bytes, saving memory and bandwidth.

The last three serve the optimizer, which picks each parameter's move. Adam keeps two numbers per parameter: the first moment tracks recent direction, so noise doesn't throw it off; the second tracks usual gradient size and sets each step: small for steadily large gradients, big for small.

So Adam's cleverness costs two numbers per parameter. With the full-precision master weights: three at four bytes, twelve of the sixteen.

Why eighteen? A step often sums gradients over many micro-batches; half precision runs out of digits, and small numbers added to big ones vanish. So Megatron accumulates in full precision by default: two becomes four, sixteen eighteen.

These sixteen bytes depend only on parameter count, not batch or sequence length. Activations scale with batch, length, width and depth. Stretch the sequence from 4K to a hundred-odd K and they grow tens of times.

So HBM holds two mountains: one follows the model, one the data. Some cuts shave the first, some the second.

Many think 8B is small: one device serves it. But inference needs only the two bytes of weights; training needs sixteen, eight times as much.

Now the other way. Over V3's 2,048 H800s, 2.788 million GPU hours is about 1,300-odd hours, under two months. Only if two thousand devices stay busy, never waiting on each other.

Two reasons to cut: to fit, split the model state; to go faster, spread the compute. Either way devices must talk.

🔍 Going deeper: why the ring is cheap, when it loses, why all-to-all is hard; the TPU 2D/3D torus (if pressed or time allows; not in the floor)

1. Why the ring is cheap

From four devices to n: n blocks, each step sends one n-th of a copy. ReduceScatter takes n − 1 steps, AllGather n − 1 more. For a thousand: 999 times two over a thousand, the opening 1.998. More devices only creep toward two.

The ring's trick: what each device sends barely depends on headcount. The root's work grows tenfold with ten times the people.

ReduceScatter costs about one copy, AllGather one. The equation holds in cost too; next section, needing one half, you pay one copy.

2. When the ring loses

We counted bytes, not steps. A thousand-device ring takes nearly two thousand steps, each waiting on the last; a tiny fixed cost per step adds up.

A collective's time has two terms: steps times fixed cost, plus data over bandwidth. Big messages: the second dominates; the ring is near optimal. Tiny ones, say one number: the first dominates; many steps are fatal.

A tree attacks the step count: a thousand devices merge in pairs, about ten rounds in, ten back out. More bytes than the ring, but twenty steps, not two thousand. So libraries pick by size: big ring, small tree.

3. Why all-to-all strains the network

Split the cluster down the middle. A ring AllReduce talks only to neighbors: two links cross the split. In AllToAll every device sends to every other: about half the data crosses. What counts is the gap's width, not one link's speed.

Machines differ. In one NVLink domain, switches link all GPUs, any to any in one hop: a wide gap. On a ring-connected machine, a letter to the far side passes several neighbors on crowded links.

More headaches. Sizes are known only after routing; devices often swap counts first. The device with an overloaded expert is extra busy; everyone waits. And letters can't merge: AllReduce sums partway and shrinks as it goes; AllToAll saves not one byte.

Last, point-to-point send/receive: one device hands data to a neighbor; of the five, easiest on the network.

4. A flat ring: the 2D torus

Sixteen chips, four rows of four, each with four links: left, right, up, down. Rows and columns wrap end to end, each a ring of four. A grid wrapped at both ends is a torus.

One big ring of sixteen works too, but uses only left and right links and takes thirty steps. The torus goes one direction at a time. First every row ReduceScatters at once; each chip keeps a quarter copy, its row's sum.

Then each column ReduceScatters that quarter: each chip keeps a sixteenth, the sum of all sixteen. The second half reverses, AllGather along columns then rows: everyone has the total.

Count. Bytes: about three quarters of a copy in the row phase; the column phase handles a quarter, sends three sixteenths. Fifteen sixteenths in all; the second half matches: about two copies, like the big ring. Steps: four phases of three, twelve, not thirty. No extra bytes, far fewer steps.

One waste: rows idle the vertical links, columns the horizontal. Halve the data, one half rows first, one columns first: all four links work. Links are bidirectional: one share each way, twice as fast again; in a ring of four the farthest chip is two hops.

A standalone AllGather or ReduceScatter runs half the recipe: AllGather small blocks first, big last; ReduceScatter the reverse, shrinking as it goes.

This assumes the rings wrap. When every dimension of a TPU slice is a multiple of four, each closes. In small slices some don't: a line, where the farthest data walks all the way, much slower.

5. A ring in 3D: the 3D torus

3D adds a direction: four by four by four, sixty-four chips, six links each. ReduceScatter along X leaves a quarter, along Y a sixteenth, along Z a sixty-fourth: the full sum. Then AllGather back along Z, Y, X.

Six small rings of three steps: eighteen. One big ring of sixty-four: a hundred and twenty-six. Bytes: still about two copies.

Idle links, same fix: split the data in three, rotate the order: X, Y, Z; Y, Z, X; Z, X, Y. All three directions work at once: triple one direction's bandwidth. The more axes a cut spans, the more links it uses; more in Section 7.

AllToAll gets no such deal on a torus. It can go one direction at a time, but letters can't merge; far ones take several hops, crowding links. The middle gap limits it, and a torus's is narrower than a big switch's. Section 4 settles that.

6. How many directions per link? Full duplex, and the v7x math

A common misconception: a ring sends on the right link and receives on the left, so each link carries one direction. In fact every ICI link is full duplex: two one-way streets, both full speed at once.

A one-way ring uses half: each chip sends right, receives from the left, leaving the right link's return lane and the left's outbound lane idle. Six links: twelve one-way streets. Rotating three pieces fills six; split each again, half each way: all twelve fill, usable bandwidth doubles again on top of 3×.

The v7x numbers. Officially, only a per-chip ICI total: 1,200 GB/s. The breakdown is our derivation: six links at 200 for both directions, 100 per direction; 600 out, 600 in, 1,200 together. External anchor: the Scaling Book's 90 GB/s per link per direction for v7. It lines up.

So for any ICI bandwidth, ask: one direction, or send plus receive? Time an AllGather with outbound bandwidth; divide by 1,200 and you come out twice too fast.

🔍 Going deeper: a ZeRO-1 step, where the accumulator counts, reduction precision, why V3 uses only ZeRO-1 (if pressed or time allows; not in the floor)

1. What does ZeRO-1 shard?

Master weights count as optimizer state: the 12 bytes are master weights 4 plus two moments at 4 each. ZeRO-1 shards all three.

Each device keeps full half-precision weights and gradients, plus its slice of master weights and moments. Slices are equal cuts of one long strip of all parameters, not whole layers.

2. How a step runs

One update per step, after backward finishes every layer, not per layer. Forward and backward use the full half-precision weights and leave a full gradient set.

Normally you'd AllReduce now. But each device owns one slice and needs no other slice's sum: it only ReduceScatters.

It updates its slice of master weights and moments and converts to half precision; only its slice of the half-precision copy is now new.

Then an AllGather: each device's new slice overwrites the old values everywhere, so all have full new weights for the next forward. The ReduceScatter can go bucket by bucket during backward; the update stays once per step.

3. Where the accumulator counts

The accumulator isn't optimizer state; it takes the gradient's slot. The 16 bytes assume one batch per step. Several micro-batches must be summed first; in half precision small ones round away, so a full-precision accumulator takes the slot: 2 bytes become 4.

ZeRO-1 doesn't shard it. For V3: half-precision weights about 1.3 TB, accumulator about 2.7 TB, exactly twice; master weights and moments split 128 ways, only tens of GB. After shaving, the accumulator is the biggest thing on the device.

Since the accumulator needs a full copy, ZeRO-2, sharding gradients, has nowhere to accumulate: it must ReduceScatter every micro-batch. With the V3 paper's precisions, its bill: weights 2, accumulator 4, master weights 4, moments 2 each; 14 total.

4. Half-precision gradient reduction?

Intuition: you've accumulated locally, so send half precision and halve the bandwidth. The catch: ReduceScatter is itself a sum. Each ring hop adds its part to the partial sum and passes it on: over a hundred additions to one number.

In half precision, every hop stores and rounds that growing partial sum, so small values added later get swallowed. The problem is storing it at each hop, not the transfer.

Both are used. Megatron's grad_reduce_in_fp32 picks full or half precision for the gradient buffer and the reduction. NVIDIA's recipes for DeepSeek-style models turn it off: half precision throughout, halving memory and bandwidth, at some accuracy cost. The V3 paper doesn't say which it uses.

It's also the main reason data parallelism and FSDP losses drift slightly apart in long runs: a different addition order, different floating-point results, amplified by training.

5. Gradient life; who shards what

Strictly, gradients needn't live as long as weights: they appear layer by layer in backward, can be zeroed after the update, and forward never uses them. But Megatron-style frameworks keep one contiguous gradient buffer all step, so the memory math counts it resident: 2 + 2 + three 4s, 16 bytes.

The accumulator doesn't sit beside the half-precision gradient; it replaces it. Each layer's half-precision gradient is added in and dropped at once; only the accumulator stays. 2 becomes 4, so 16 becomes 18, not 20.

Who shards what: ZeRO-1, only those 12 bytes; the accumulator, in the gradient slot, stays. ZeRO-2 shards the gradients, accumulator included. ZeRO-3, i.e. FSDP, adds the weights; all three split 128 ways.

In stages that shard gradients, when backward reaches a layer, its full gradient appears briefly on every device. A ReduceScatter leaves each device its 1/128 of the sum and frees the rest. With micro-batches, that repeats per micro-batch, into the device's accumulator slice.

6. Which stage does V3 actually use?

The V3 paper: 16-way pipeline parallelism, 64-way expert parallelism, ZeRO-1 data parallelism. 2048 devices ÷ 16 stages = 128-way data parallelism, the lecture's 128. But those 128 ways run ZeRO-1, not FSDP.

ZeRO-3 includes ZeRO-2, so it inherits the micro-batch problem, worse. Besides a ReduceScatter per micro-batch, it re-gathers every layer's weights in forward and backward, and a pipeline step runs many micro-batches.

Memory comes from the other cuts: 16-way pipelining leaves 1/16 of the layers per device; 64-way EP spreads the experts. ZeRO-1 takes the largest piece left, optimizer state, at no extra communication. The main text's 128-way FSDP at 84 GB/device is a data-parallel-only what-if showing that alone isn't enough, not V3's real setup.

Also remember: data parallelism talks once per step, FSDP hundreds or thousands of times. The chattier it is, the more it needs the fast lane; more in Section 7.

What does this cut leave open? Each step FSDP moves the weights, an amount set by model size alone. Compute scales with each device's tokens; a token is roughly a word.
Add devices, and the total fed in at once can't grow without limit (too big, and the model learns worse), so each device gets fewer.
Less compute, same traffic: moving weights eats all the time, forcing the second cut.

How few is “few”? Simple question: move something once, how often is it used?

🔍 Going deeper: what was new in GPipe (if pressed or time allows; not in the floor)

1. Not pipelining, but making it synchronous

GPipe is a 2018 Google paper. Relaying layer stages was old; GPipe made pipelining synchronous and general. The core is this section's trick: cut a batch into micro-batches, feed them in turn, accumulate their gradients, update once at the end.

Mathematically, the result equals no pipeline: one big device start to finish. Microsoft's PipeDream, same era, went asynchronous, micro-batches on different weight versions: no bubble, but a different result and convergence worries. GPipe kept the result; the price is the bubble.

2. Tied to recomputation

Each stage keeps only its boundary activations; inner ones are dropped after forward and recomputed in backward: a little compute for a lot of activation memory. Pairing it with pipelining, GPipe popularized today's activation checkpointing.

3. Bubble math, and the scale then

The paper's conclusion: at least four times as many micro-batches as stages, the bubble is negligible; hence the course page's rule of thumb. They trained a 6-billion-parameter, 128-layer Transformer with it, translating over a hundred languages.

The static figure is GPipe's schedule: all forwards, then all backwards. Later 1F1B: same bubble, less activation memory.

What this cut leaves open starts with an oddity: V3 trained with no TP at all; the report says memory was optimized enough without it.
And the parameters? Nearly all sit in narrow “expert” networks. Cut a very narrow matrix and each device gets even less work, yet passes as many panels: why the flat line dropped.
So don't split matrices; split by expert.

🔍 Going deeper: Ulysses vs. TP; V3 step by step; when Ulysses, when USP; Ulysses vs Ring (if pressed or time allows; not in the floor time)

1. Inside attention, it's exactly TP

After the swap, each device computes a few heads over the whole sequence: exactly TP's shape on attention. It doesn't “degenerate into TP” with one chunk: chunks and head groups both equal the device count, so heads can't split without the sequence splitting.

2. The whole difference is outside attention

Three things. Weights: TP splits them by head; each device uses only its heads' projections. Ulysses projects, then swaps: each device computes all heads' Q, K, V for its tokens, so at the moment of use it needs every head's projection weights. Alone it saves activations, not weights; the paper adds ZeRO-3: weights sharded, AllGathered when needed.
Outside attention: each TP device holds the full sequence; a Ulysses device, only its chunk, with full weights. The MLP moves not one byte.
What's sent: TP, partial sums, an AllReduce after the output projection over the whole sequence's activations. Ulysses, the activations, each device one over the device count of its share.

Closest relative: Megatron's TP plus sequence parallelism, which AllGathers the whole sequence's input to every device before attention. Ulysses computes Q, K, V locally, then swaps. The paper compares exactly this: Megatron sends 4Nh per device per layer, Ulysses 4Nh ÷ device count.

3. Walking through V3: 128 heads, a million tokens, 128 devices

V3's config has 128 heads. Set MLA aside; pretend each head's Q, K, V are 128 dims. Take 2 to the 20th tokens, just over 1,048,000, cut into 128 chunks of exactly 8,192, one per device.

Step one: each device projects its 8,192 tokens into Q, K, V with all heads' weights, so it holds all 128 heads for its own chunk. Q alone is 8,192 × 128 heads × 128 dims, 256 MiB in half precision; K and V likewise.

Step two: the first AllToAll. Each device cuts Q by head into 128 blocks, 8,192 tokens × one head, 2 MiB each. Head n goes to device n, its own kept. Same for K, V.
Device 5 now has one block from each of the 128 devices: all head 5, chunks 0 through 127. Join them in chunk order: head 5's Q, K, V over the whole million tokens. That's the concatenation; order matters.

Step three: device 5 runs FlashAttention with the causal mask for head 5, asking no one. The million-squared score matrix is computed only block by block. Bonus: identical work per device; the causal mask leaves no one busier.

Step four: the second AllToAll retraces the route. Device 5 cuts head 5's output into 128 chunk blocks, chunk n to device n.
Now device 0 holds heads 0 through 127, all for chunk 0. Join them along the head dimension: chunk 0's complete attention output. That's the merge.

Step five, back to normal: device 0's full output projection compresses 128 heads to 7,168 dims; residual, LayerNorm and MLP finish its 8,192 tokens locally. Repeat per layer.

4. The numbers

Per device per layer, outbound: Q, K, V and output, four 256 MiB tensors, 127 blocks of each sent. About 1 GiB, exactly 4Nh divided by 128. Backward: the same again.
Versus 128-way TP: each device holds all million tokens, so one layer's input activations alone are 14 GiB. Each AllReduce sums that 14 GiB; on a ring each device sends nearly twice that, once for attention, once for the MLP.

In one line: inside attention, Ulysses is TP by head; but it projects before swapping (needing every head's weights), and outside attention handles only its chunk. Price: one swap before, one after, each moving one over the device count.

5. When to use Ulysses, and when to combine it into USP

Weights first: Ulysses doesn't save them; that's ZeRO-3's or FSDP's job. The paper's section 3.3 merges the sequence-parallel group into the ZeRO-3 sharding group; the video example below pairs FSDP with USP. So its limits are head count and network, not weights.

Fits: tiny batch, very long sequence; precisely, a single sample doesn't fit on one device. Data parallelism splits by sample, stopping at one per device; below that, split inside the sample. Short sequences, big batches: data parallelism suffices.

Case one: long-context training. Extend a model from thousands of tokens to hundreds of thousands or a million, and no device holds one sample's activations. Ulysses handles activations, ZeRO-3 weights.
Case two: video-generation inference. The Wan 2.1 README runs 8-GPU inference as FSDP plus USP, Ulysses = 8 for the 14B model. One request, one clip: batch 1, and high-resolution video is a very long token sequence. Attention is bidirectional: no causal mask, no KV cache. The user wants this one request fast, beyond data parallelism's help, so the sequence is split across eight GPUs.
The same README has a counterexample: the 1.3B model has only 12 heads, not divisible by 8, so its 8-GPU runs switch to Ring.

Doesn't fit: LLM decode, one token per step, nothing long to split; splitting the KV cache is DCP's job (later). And TP, if on, also splits by head, leaving Ulysses fewer heads.

Use USP in three situations.
More devices than heads. The USP paper's example: Llama 3 8B has only 8 KV heads, so 16-way means Ulysses 8 × Ring 2.
Heads don't divide. For that 12-head model the README recommends pure Ring; by USP's rules Ulysses 4 × Ring 2 also works (4 divides 12): derived from the rules, not in the README.
Crossing machines. AllToAll hates slow links: keep the Ulysses dimension on one machine's 8 devices, over NVLink. Ring, neighbor-only and overlapping, goes between.

Back to V3, counting bytes (per device per layer, forward, head dims as assumed). Pure 128-way Ulysses: about 1 GiB, under 1 GiB of it cross-machine. USP with Ulysses 8 inside and Ring 16 across: the cross-machine ring alone is about 7.5 GiB.
So with 128 heads to go around and a network that handles the AllToAll, pure Ulysses sends least. USP is the fallback when heads run short or cross-machine AllToAll can't run at full speed. Formula-derived, not measured.

In one line: a sample doesn't fit and one request must be faster, Ulysses; heads run short or you cross machines, USP. Weights always go to FSDP or ZeRO-3.

6. What Ulysses is really good at, compared with Ring

Misconception: AllToAll sends more. The reverse. Same V3 example, per device per layer, forward: under Ring each device collects the other 127 chunks' K and V, about 64 GiB. Ulysses: about 1 GiB, over sixty times less.
Why? Ring: each device always sees the whole sequence's K and V, about 2Nh, at any device count. Ulysses: each swaps one over the device count of its share, 4Nh divided by the device count. More devices, less sent.

Three more strengths.
Attention in one piece. After the swap each device has one head over the whole sequence: off-the-shelf FlashAttention works, sparse and sliding-window variants unchanged. Ring merges softmax block by block: a custom kernel, and small blocks can't saturate compute.
Balanced by construction: one head over the whole sequence each, identical work. Under a causal mask, Ring leaves later chunks busy, earlier ones idle, unless you shuffle the sequence.
Fewer steps: two AllToAlls per layer, before and after; Ring passes 127 times.

Ring's two strengths: no head-count cap, and communication overlapped with compute. Compute grows with the square of length, so at very long sequences each step computes far more than it sends, hiding those sixty-plus GiB. Ulysses' AllToAll can't hide: compute waits on it, on the critical path.
So at 128 devices and a million tokens, plain Ring works; compute probably covers communication. Price: sixty-plus times more bytes on the NICs, fighting data and expert parallelism for links. With enough heads and a network that handles the AllToAll, Ulysses is cheaper.

🔍 Going deeper: how placement is written on TPU: GSPMD vs. plain SPMD (if pressed or time allows; not in the floor time)

1. Plain SPMD: you write each device's program

SPMD, a programming model: single program, multiple data. Every device runs the same program on its own data. Plain style: you hand-write each device's piece, when to talk, which AllReduce to call. Megatron's TP was written that way.

2. GSPMD: you write one big device's program; the compiler splits it

GSPMD: a 2021 Google paper and an XLA compiler feature. Write for one big device; annotate a few key tensors: shard this dimension along that mesh axis. The compiler propagates the annotations, picks each step's sharding, inserts collectives like AllGather and ReduceScatter, and emits one program for every device.

Two payoffs. Generality: data, tensor, FSDP and expert parallelism are just different annotations; model code stays put. Section 3's two-by-four FSDP-by-TP grid is annotated so on TPU. Scale: any device count, one compiled program, so compile time doesn't grow.

3. How it ties to this section

On TPU, this section's placement lives here: lay the chips out as a multi-dimensional mesh, then pick which axis is TP, which FSDP. Wrong axis: placement 2. JAX's jit-plus-sharding and MaxText sit on it. Its predecessor GShard trained a 600-billion-parameter MoE translation model in 2020.

In short: plain SPMD, you write each device's program; GSPMD, one big device's, split by the compiler.

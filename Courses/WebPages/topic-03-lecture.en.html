Section 1 · A chain (7′)

🎯 Leave one shape in their heads: a fixed-size box. It comes back unchanged in Section 7, the lecture's climax.

Let's go back to 1990. The answer back then was almost crudely simple: carry a box. Read a word, mix it into the box, write it back. Read the next word, mix again. At the end, what's in the box is all the model knows about the passage.

⭐⭐ Spend thirty seconds unpacking the name. ⛔ The lecture says “RNN” from the first line but never spells it out. (Asked 2026-09-15.) ⭐ After the move, so the room recognizes the R from the picture. ⛔ Not in the opening: no picture yet.

While we're here, the three letters: RNN, Recurrent Neural Network.
⭐ The R refers to the move you just heard. After the mix, the box doesn't leave. It loops back as the next step's input. That loop is where the name comes from.
⭐ The word is plain, too. “Recurrent” is Latin: re-, back; currere, to run. Literally, running back.

⭐⭐ Little known: mathematicians didn't coin the word. It came from anatomy.
There, “recurrent” had long described looping structures. Cajal saw them in the cerebellar cortex in 1901; Lorente de Nó found more in 1933.
⛔ Before that, the nervous system was treated as purely feed-forward. Loops in the brain weren't seriously proposed until the 1940s.

⭐ This echoes in Section 7, where Albert Gu says “this branch is like a brain.” Borrowed from the brain, thirty years later borrowed back. ⛔ Not now.

⛔ And don't confuse it with a recursive neural network.
That's a different thing: it drills down a parse tree.
⭐ An RNN is recurrent: it loops along the time axis. Recursive drills down. One circles in place; the other digs.

⭐⭐⭐ Added 2026-09-16, after three questions in a row: “What mechanism mixes it in?” “Why doesn't mixing scramble what's there?” “Was KV invented here?” The course answered none.
⛔ Root cause: “mixing” suggests blending, which destroys. ⭐ A metaphor that plants a wrong physical intuition owes the room a figure.

A moment ago I said “mix.” That metaphor kept it simple, but it misleads you.
⛔ “Mix” makes you think of blending. And blending destroys things.
⭐ So let's open the box and look.

⭐ Ⓐ. Inside one step, there are really just four moves.
① Rotate: multiply the previous box by a learned matrix. It turns to a new direction.
② Map: multiply the new word by another matrix, into the same space.
③ Add: add the two.
④ Squash: a nonlinearity pulls it back into range. Then write it back into the box.

⭐⭐ Step three matters most: it's an add, not a blend.
The new stacks on top of the old. Nothing old gets erased.

⛔ Ask the counter-question yourself.

⛔ The obvious question: after ten thousand adds, can you still pull them apart?

⭐⭐⭐ Ⓑ, left half first. This is the heart of the figure: not a claim, a number you can compute.
In two dimensions, two random directions are typically only about fifty degrees apart. Stack them and they blur, beyond recovery.
But in 1,024 dimensions, two random directions are 88.6 degrees apart.

⭐ Almost perpendicular.
Two things stacked at nearly right angles can each be pulled back out.
⭐⭐ The line: superposition is recoverable because dimensions pay for it.

⚠️ Give the limit at once, or it sounds like “more dimensions fix all.”

⛔ But it's not unlimited: there are at most d directions that truly don't interfere. Overfill it and it blurs anyway.
⭐⭐ Hold on to that. It comes back once more today, to explain why another notepad fills up.

⭐ Ⓑ, right half. Why does it still fade with distance?
Because every step multiplies it again.
Step one to step one hundred: multiplied, and squashed, ninety-nine times.

⛔ If the matrix's “gain” is a bit under one, a hundred multiplies shrink it to zero exponentially; a bit over, it blows up exponentially.
⭐⭐ That's the whole mechanism of fading: not washed out by time, but multiplied away.

⭐⭐⭐ The lecture's best early echo. Pause first.

⭐ A note for the end of today:
“multiply by less than one at every step” was this approach's fatal flaw in 1990.
Today's branch put it back in, on purpose.
One difference: back then the decay was uncontrolled. Today it's learned, one per channel.
⭐⭐ Same property. Once it's the disease, once it's the cure.

⭐⭐⭐ The takeaway band. Last, an important question: was KV invented here?
⛔ No. The box holds neither.
The 1990 box holds a single vector. No keys, no content.

⭐ Keys and content as a pair came with attention. The names Q, K and V had to wait until 2017.
The KV cache as “a bill to pay” came later still. Only when autoregressive generation made recomputing the whole history every step cost real money did anyone cache it.

⭐⭐ The next two lines land this section; then to “the box is always the same size.” ⛔ Don't skip: the ruler for the next eight sections; else each is “one more method.”

⭐⭐ Remember these two; they're the book's skeleton:
RNN state = all history squeezed into one blob.
KV cache = all history kept verbatim.

⭐⭐ The next three came from 2026-09-16 follow-ups. ⛔ The second answer is “the paper doesn't say”: present the silence as the answer. Don't supply a number.

⭐ Three questions you may have:
First, how long is that box, really? Smaller than you'd imagine.
In Elman's 1990 paper, the largest simulation used 150 numbers, the two letter-prediction ones 20, the smallest, XOR, two.

⭐⭐ Today, one linear-attention notepad holds tens of thousands of numbers per head, per layer. With full attention, a long-context KV cache runs to gigabytes.
That's the jump from box to notepad.

Second, what precision? The paper doesn't say.
⭐ That's part of the answer: back then, precision wasn't something you traded off. It was just the machine's ordinary floating point. Nobody treated it as a knob.
⭐⭐ Precision became worth discussing much later.
When a quantity starts showing up in papers is itself a signal: that's when it became something to trade off.

And two you'll surely think of: where are the learned weights, and how big is the matrix that does the mixing?
⭐ Just three learned matrices: one maps the new word in, one rotates the previous state, one reads the answer out. Plus biases.

⭐⭐ The sizes are in the figure. The sentence simulation: 31-dimensional input, 150-dimensional state.
The map-in matrix is 31 by 150: 4,650 numbers.
The rotate matrix is 150 by 150: 22,500 numbers.
The readout is another 4,650.

⭐⭐⭐ These two get an audible “oh.” Don't rush.

⛔ Surprise: the loop itself isn't trained.
The paper is blunt: “Recurrent connections are fixed at 1.0 and are not subject to adjustment.” It just copies this step's state to the next, unchanged.
⭐ What's learned is the matrix that reads the copy back in. And it holds seventy percent of all the parameters.

⭐⭐ For scale: the whole model has just over thirty thousand parameters.
Not thirty trillion, not three billion. Thirty thousand.

⭐⭐⭐ The most valuable line here: it explains what embeddings are for.

⭐ You'll ask: what did “word vectors” look like back then? How many bits?
⛔ Answer: there was no “embedding.”
Elman gave each word a 31-bit vector, one bit per word. (Two bits were reserved for later experiments: the real vocabulary was 29 words.)
Assigned at random, never trained.

⭐⭐ The paper names two consequences:
First, the word vectors are mutually orthogonal.
Second, they carry no part of speech or meaning.

⭐⭐⭐ Those two sentences say exactly what embeddings were later for:
Then: perfectly orthogonal, but zero information.
Today: learned dense vectors, only nearly orthogonal, but carrying meaning.
⭐ Whether “nearly orthogonal” suffices is what the 88.6 degrees answered.

⭐⭐⭐ Added after four questions in a row on 2026-09-16. ⛔ Pause on the “8,000 numbers”: the cheapest scale comparison in the lecture.

⭐ Once usable, how big was the box? Elman's 150 was a toy.
The first truly usable large RNN was the 2014 machine-translation paper: four layers, 1,000 cells per layer, 1,000-dimensional word vectors, a 160,000-word input vocabulary, 80,000 on output.

⭐⭐ The very next sentence is the gem:
“The deep LSTM uses 8000 real numbers to represent a sentence.”
To this model, a whole sentence is eight thousand numbers.

⛔ Set that against the opening question:
a 488 GiB KV cache, stored in bf16, is 262 billion numbers.
⭐⭐⭐ Eight thousand to 262 billion: 32.75 million times.
That's the real gap between “squeezed into one blob” and “kept verbatim.”

⭐⭐⭐ The best of the four. Go slowly.

⭐ So what does each of the RNN's three matrices extract?
For attention you know it: Q is what I'm looking for, K is what I can answer, V is what I hand over.
⛔ The RNN's answer: no such division of labor.
⭐⭐ Not for lack of study: the architecture never separated the roles.

The input matrix translates the word into state space.
Forced into an analogy, it's K and V mixed: what's written is both the content and its later lookup key, one thing.
The recurrent matrix does two jobs: keeping (carrying the old state forward) and transforming (what grows, what decays). So it's both keeper and retriever of memory.

⭐⭐⭐ This is exactly why attention was a breakthrough:
it split “what to remember” and “how to find it” into two independent parameterizations.
In an RNN, both jobs are one matrix moonlighting.
⭐ Q, K and V mean something clean because retrieval was written into the architecture. An RNN has no retrieval step; it only mixes.

⭐ So what are x and y?
x is the current word's vector. h is the state after all prior words.
y is the state through the output matrix: a vector as long as the vocabulary. After softmax: each word's odds of coming next.
⭐⭐ So yes, it's next-token prediction.
Elman says so: “the task on each input cycle is to predict the next word in the sequence.”
1990. The same training objective, still used 35 years on.

⭐⭐ This passage had its own panel (Ⓐb, two loops) until 2026-09-16. ⛔ It covers Section 1's pain and Section 2's fix, not “what's in the box,” so the full version is in 1.2 and Section 2; here, one line. ⚠️ The session's intuition (“shouldn't y loop back as the next x?”) is right; state it as two loops, or you'll get teacher forcing wrong.

Section 2 · The 2017 cut (10′)

🎯 Leave behind: the KV cache is born here.

⭐⭐ 2.1 is the lecture's only human story. Three minutes; read the original words.

The answer is: yes, and in 2017 someone actually did it.
⛔ But first, something many people get wrong: attention was not invented in 2017.
It already existed in 2014. And at first, it was a small add-on bolted onto an RNN.

So the cut didn't invent attention. It threw away the chain the add-on was bolted to, and kept only the add-on.
But before the cut, let me tell you where the add-on came from. Nobody had a flash of inspiration. It was complained into existence, one version at a time.

⭐ Say the name once; after that, “the intern.” Foreign names are forgotten; “an intern” sticks, and who he was is half the story.

In 2014, Bengio's lab had an intern named Bahdanau.
He tried two designs, liking neither. The first was too complex to work; the second worked, but dumbly, pairing words in order: third source word to third translated word.
Then one day…

⚠️ Before the quote, half a sentence of setup, or “decoder” is new to the room: “When he says ‘the decoder,’ he means the half that's writing out the translation.”

So one day I had this thought that it would be nice to enable the decoder RNN to learn to search where to put the cursor in the source sequence. This was sort of inspired by translation exercises that learning English in my middle school involved. Your gaze shifts back and forth between source and target sequence as you translate.

⭐ After the quote, pause a beat, then this line. It turns a term back into human experience.

So, that's where attention comes from. Not some grand theory. A student remembering how his eyes moved while he did his translation homework.

⭐⭐⭐ Softmax appears here first. Give it a plain line now: it's the root of the bug in Section 6. ⛔ Wait till then and the room meets a new mechanism and term at once.

He wrote this ‘soft search’ as something called softmax.
⭐ Think of softmax as one sentence: it turns a pile of scores into percentages that add up to exactly 100%.
Remember that: “adds up to exactly 100%.” Later today it causes real trouble.

It worked on the very first try.
He called it RNNSearch. Later he said: the name was not great.
The word “attention” was added by Bengio in one of the final passes.

⭐ Bengio's “why it has to be soft” is the foundation of the lecture. Read all of it. It explains every later softmax and plants the Section 6 bug.

This would be like first reading the whole book in French, then, having the story in mind, generating the English translation of the book. We would for sure forget some details. The solution was to allow looking back at any piece of detail in the French book while producing every additional word in the English translation. To make the whole thing compatible with traditional training with backpropagation, we had to make the attention ‘soft’: instead of picking a single location of focus, we have a total budget of attention that can be split across all the locations.

⭐ Notice his last line: “a total budget of attention that can be split across all the locations.”
That's the softmax from a minute ago, in plain words.
⭐⭐ And it's a strength: because the total is fixed and can be split, the thing is trainable.
⛔ Hold on to this. In Section 6, it becomes the root of a bug. ⭐ Same line as in Section 1 (just a preview; its real second outing is Section 6):

To follow this section, accept one premise: all four schemes share one shape.

So why copy at all?

⭐ GQA saves warehouse space: only eight cups. The price: sixteen heads must share one cup. They can no longer look at different things.

The other two sit at either end.

First, a misreading: you don't add the token's vector straight onto the notepad.
⛔ Shapes don't match: the notepad is a d-by-d square; a token's vector, a line of length d.

The real move has two steps.
⭐ Step one: split it into a pair. Two learned matrices project the token into a key k and a content v, each a line of length d.
⭐ Step two: weld them into a card. Take their outer product: two length-d lines make a d-by-d square.
What goes onto the notepad is the card, not the parts. Now the shapes match.

⭐⭐ Once it's all one blob, how can you still pull out a single card?
The key: reading is linear too. Querying with a key = the notepad times that key.
The notepad is a sum of cards, so that product splits apart on its own: each card's content, weighted by how similar its key is to yours.

⛔ So what comes out always has two parts.
Part one: the card you wanted, whose key matches yours exactly.
Part two: a little leakage from every other card, scaled by how similar their keys are to yours.

⭐⭐⭐ Part two is crosstalk. It's the “smearing” coming up shortly.
If all keys are mutually orthogonal, crosstalk is exactly zero: a clean read.
But a d-dimensional space has at most d mutually orthogonal directions.
⭐ That's the source of “the notepad only holds d entries.” Not a metaphor: you can check it.
